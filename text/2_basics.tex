\section{Motivation for Feature Selection}
\label{sec:motivation-feature-selection}
% Feature selection method - page 19
One of the fundamental motivations for feature selection is to deal with a \textit{curse of dimensionality}, which refers to a collection of phenomena that arise when analyzing data in high-dimensional spaces, where the number of features 
$p$ is large. In such settings, the geometry of the space changes in a way that makes many standard statistical and machine learning methods less effective.

As illustrated by the authors in  \cite{hastie2001}; the curse of dimensionality becomes particularly clear when analyzing how neighborhoods behave in high-dimensional spaces. Consider inputs uniformly distributed in a 
$p$-dimensional unit hypercube $[0,1]^p$, as in Figure~\ref{fig:hypercube}. We define a local neighborhood as a smaller $p$-dimensional hypercube, such that it contains a fixed fraction $r \in (0,1)$ of the total volume of the space.

To achieve this, the edge length of the neighborhood hypercube must satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_p(r) = r^{1/p}.
\end{equation}

This relationship has a counterintuitive implication: as the dimension $p$ increases, the required edge length grows rapidly. In particular, the side length $\varepsilon_p(r)$ approaches $1$ as $p \to \infty$, even for small values of $r$. This means that to capture a fixed proportion of the data, one must include a large fraction of each variable’s range.

For example, in ten dimensions, retaining only $1\%$ of the total volume requires an edge length of $0.01^{1/10} \approx 0.63,$
while retaining $10\%$ requires approximately $0.1^{1/10} \approx 0.80.$ 

This phenomenon highlights a key issue in high-dimensional data analysis: points that are in low-dimensional settings may become effectively distant when embedded in higher-dimensional spaces. As a consequence, data becomes increasingly sparse, meaning that most regions of the space contain few or no observations. This sparsity makes local methods less reliable and significantly complicates tasks such as distance-based learning, clustering, and density estimation in high dimensions.

\section{Feature Selection: Formal Definition and Feature Types}

\url{https://ift6758.github.io/lectures/feature_selection.pdfx}

\emph{Feature selection} addresses these issues by reducing the dimensionality of the input space.

Let $(\mathbf{X}, Y) \sim P$, where $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is a random vector of features and $Y$ is the target variable. Let $P$ denote their joint probability distribution.

Realizations of $\mathbf{X}$ and $Y$ are denoted by $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_p) \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and $y$, respectively.

The goal of feature selection is to identify a subset of feature indices $S \subseteq \{1, \dots, p\}$ such that $|S| = k \ll p$. For a given subset $S$, define the reduced random feature vector
\[
\mathbf{X}_S = (X_j)_{j \in S},
\]
whose realization is denoted by $\mathbf{x}_S \in \mathbb{R}^k$, obtained by selecting the components of $\mathbf{x}$ indexed by $S$. The learning algorithm is then applied to \( \mathbf{x}_S \) instead of the full feature vector \( \mathbf{x} \).

The goal of feature selection is to identify informative features and remove those
that do not contribute to prediction.

A feature \( X_j \) is said to be \emph{relevant} if it provides additional information about \( Y \), i.e.
\begin{equation}
P(Y \mid \mathbf{X}) \neq P(Y \mid \mathbf{X}_{-j}),
\end{equation}
where \( \mathbf{X}_{-j} \) denotes the random vector with the \( j \)-th component removed.

A feature \( X_j \) is \emph{irrelevant} if it does not affect the conditional distribution of \( Y \), i.e.
\begin{equation}
P(Y \mid \mathbf{X}) = P(Y \mid \mathbf{X}_{-j}).
\end{equation}

A feature \( X_j \) is \emph{redundant} if its information is fully contained in a subset of other features \( S \subseteq \{1, \ldots, p\} \setminus \{j\} \), i.e.
\begin{equation}
P(Y \mid X_j, \mathbf{X}_S) = P(Y \mid \mathbf{X}_S).
\end{equation}

Removing irrelevant features reduces noise, while removing redundant features eliminates unnecessary duplication of information. Both contribute to a more compact and efficient representation of the data.



\section{Feature Selection vs.\ Feature Extraction}

Feature selection and feature extraction are two approaches to dimensionality reduction.

\subsection*{Common properties}
XXXXXXXXXXXXXX
Both feature selection and feature extraction aim to reduce the dimensionality of the data, which decreases storage and computational requirements. By simplifying the input space, they can improve the performance of learning algorithms, particularly in high-dimensional settings. Additionally, both approaches support the construction of models that generalize better to unseen data.
XXXXXXXXXXXXXXXX
Common feature selection methods include filter approaches such as mutual information and correlation-based selection, wrapper methods that evaluate subsets of features using predictive models, and embedded methods like Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) or decision tree–based selection. These approaches will be discussed in more detail in \textcolor{blue}{Section 2.4}. 
XXXXXXXXXXX
Common feature extraction methods include principal component analysis (PCA), independent component analysis (ICA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and autoencoders. While the underlying approaches differ, both families of methods aim to capture the most relevant information in a smaller representation of the data.

\subsection*{Differences}

The key difference lies in how the reduced representation is constructed.

Feature selection chooses a subset of the original features. Formally, it defines a projection
\[
\pi_S : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^k, \quad \pi_S(x) = (x_j)_{j \in S}.
\]
The original variables are preserved, which maintains their physical meaning and allows direct interpretation of the model.

Feature extraction constructs new features as transformations of the original ones. It defines a mapping
\[
\phi : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^k,
\]
where the components of $\phi(x)$ are typically functions of all input variables (e.g.\ linear combinations). In this case, the original meaning of individual features is generally lost.

As a result, feature selection is preferred when interpretability is important, while feature extraction is more suitable when the goal is maximal dimensionality reduction or capturing complex structure in the data.

\section{Applications of Feature Selection}
Feature selection becomes particularly important in datasets with high dimensionality, noisy features, or a small number of samples relative to the number of features. High-dimensional datasets often contain irrelevant or redundant features, which can reduce model performance and increase computational cost. 
For example, in genomics, a single experiment can measure tens of thousands of gene expression levels for only a few hundred samples. In such cases, most features are either irrelevant or occur with very low frequency, making it difficult for learning algorithms to identify meaningful patterns. 
Similarly, text classification datasets may contain thousands of words that contribute little to the predictive task. Feature selection mitigates these issues by identifying a subset of informative and non-redundant features.

\section{Feature Selection Algorithms}
\textbf{Based on label availability} \\
Feature selection methods can be classified by whether they use label information during selection. \emph{Supervised methods} rely on labeled data to identify features that best predict the target, as in classification or regression. \emph{Unsupervised methods} do not use labels and instead select features based on intrinsic properties of the data, such as variance or similarity, making them suitable for clustering or exploratory analysis. \emph{Semi-supervised} methods combine labeled and unlabeled data, exploiting the small amount of labeled information while leveraging the structure in the unlabeled data. In this thesis, we focus exclusively on supervised feature selection methods.  

\textbf{Based on output type} \\
Feature selection methods can also be categorized according to the form of their output. Some methods return a \emph{subset of features}, typically represented as a vector of selected feature indices. Others produce a \emph{rank of features}, often as an ordered list indicating their relative importance. A third type of output consists of \emph{feature weights}, where each feature is assigned a relevance score; these weights can subsequently be used to derive a ranking by ordering features according to their values.

\textbf{Univariate or multivariate methods} \\
Feature selection methods can also be classified as univariate or multivariate (Lazar et al., 2012). Univariate methods evaluate each feature independently with respect to the target variable, ignoring dependencies between features. These approaches are typically simple and computationally efficient, but they may fail to capture feature interactions. In contrast, multivariate methods evaluate features jointly and account for dependencies among them. While these methods are generally more complex, they tend to be more robust as they can capture feature interactions and synergy.

\textbf{Selection strategies} \\
From the perspective of selection strategy, feature selection algorithms can be categorized into wrapper, filter, and embedded methods, with additional approaches including hybrid and ensemble methods ([1]).

\subsection{Wrapper methods} 
Wrapper methods perform a search over the space of possible feature subsets to find the one that optimizes a predictive model’s performance. Formally, each candidate subset \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}\) is assessed using a cost function \(\mathcal{L}(S)\), , which represents an empirical estimate of the prediction error of a model trained using only the features in $S$.

Let $\mathbf{x}_i^{(S)}$ denote the feature vector of the $i$-th observation restricted to the indices in $S$. For a fixed model $f_S$, the empirical risk can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(S) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\bigl(y_i, f_S(\mathbf{x}_i^{(S)})\bigr),
\end{equation}

where $\ell(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a task-dependent loss function, $y_i$ is the observed target value, and $f_S$ is a model trained using only the subset of features $S$.

In practice, $\mathcal{L}(S)$ is often computed using cross-validation in order to obtain a more reliable estimate of the generalization error, especially when the dataset is small or the model has high variance. Typical choices of the loss function $\ell$ depend on the learning task, such as misclassification error for classification problems or mean squared error for regression problems.

The objective of the wrapper approach is then to solve
\begin{equation}
S^* = \arg\min_{S \subset \{1,\dots,d\}} \mathcal{L}(S).
\end{equation}

These methods evaluate subsets according to a search strategy, which determines the order and selection of candidate subsets. These methods proceed iteratively: in each step, a candidate subset is evaluated, and the search is updated accordingly. 

The iterations continue until a stopping criterion is satisfied, such as a fixed number of iterations, a maximum number of selected features, or convergence in the improvement of the objective function.

Different search strategies exist, ranging from \emph{forward or backward selection} to more sophisticated heuristic or metaheuristic approaches. These will be discussed in the XXXXXXXX.

Wrapper methods are model-agnostic, meaning they can be applied to any predictive model. However, they are computationally expensive, since each evaluation of a feature subset requires training and validating at least one model. This leads to a significantly higher computational cost compared to filter-based methods \cite{guyon2006}. In this thesis, we examine \emph{Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE)} as an example of a wrapper method. 

\subsection{Filter methods}
Filter methods are independent of any learning algorithm and rely on intrinsic properties of the data. They usually rank or score features using statistical or information-theoretic criteria such as correlation, chi-squared statistics, mutual information, or information gain. Filter methods are computationally efficient and often serve as a preprocessing step.

\subsection{Embedded methods}
Embedded methods perform feature selection during the training of the model. These methods are part of the model either as built-in functionality or via regularization. Common embedded approaches include decision tree--based algorithms such as Classifcation and Regression Trees (CART), and Random Forest, as well as variants of multinomial logistic regression. Some embedded methods apply regularization to perform feature weighting, forcing coefficients to shrink or become exactly zero, as in LASSO or Elastic Net. Embedded methods inherit the merits of wrapper and filter methods: they account for feature interactions with the learning algorithm and are generally more efficient than wrappers. 


\section{Search strategies}
A \textit{search strategy} defines the order in which the variable subsets are evaluated. These strategies are particularly important for wrapper methods. Few of the search strategies are proposed by Guyon et al. \cite{guyon2006}. Most common strategies are forward selection, backward elimination, floating search, and randomized search. 

\textit{Forward selection} starts with an empty feature set and adds features one at a time, keeping those that improve the model's performance. \textit{Backward elimination} starts with all features and removes them iteratively, discarding those that contribute least to the predictive power.

Both methods are considered \cite{exhaustive searches}, as they evaluate all possible subsets of features up to a certain size. For datasets with many features, forward and backward selection can be computationally expensive. In forward selection, if the optimal number of features $k$ is unknown, one may need to evaluate all subsets of size $1$ to $k$, requiring $\binom{N}{1} + \binom{N}{2} + \dots + \binom{N}{k}$ combinations, which gives a time complexity of $O(2^N)$. 

For this reason, more efficient algorithms have been developed, such as \emph{sequential forward selection (SFS)} and \emph{sequential backward selection (SBS)}. SFS starts with an empty feature set and iteratively adds the feature, that yields the greatest improvement in model performance, while previously selected features remain selected. SBS is similar to SFS, but it starts with the full feature set and iteratively removes the feature that contributes the least. Both SFS and SBS are heuristic, thus they do not guarantee the optimal subset. Research also shows that exhaustive search is more prone to overfitting, than sequential methods. \cite{guyon2006}

\emph{Floating search} extends forward or backward selection by allowing conditional inclusion or exclusion of features. \emph{Sequential Forward Floating Selection} (SFFS) can remove previously added features if they later become redundant, and \emph{Sequential Backward Floating Selection} (SBFS) can re-add previously removed features, making the search more flexible.

\emph{Randomized search} explores the subset space probabilistically, using heuristics or metaheuristics such as genetic algorithms.

\section{Classical Methods}

\subsection{Kruskal Wallis Filter}

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) represents a class of statistical methods used to test whether multiple groups originate from the same distribution.The method is based on decomposing total variability into within-group and between-group components. Formally, the ANOVA test statistic can be expressed as the ratio of within-group to between-group variation.

However, ANOVA relies on the assumption of normally distributed data. In cases where this assumption is violated, a nonparametric alternative is required. For this reason, we employ the Kruskal–Wallis H test, which does not assume normality and is based on rank statistics.

The Kruskal--Wallis test statistic is given by:
\[
H = \frac{12}{n(n+1)} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{R_i^2}{n_i} - 3(n+1),
\]
where \(k\) denotes the number of groups, \(n\) is the total number of observations, \(n_i\) is the number of observations in the \(i\)-th group, and \(R_i\) represents the sum of ranks for the \(i\)-th group.

Each feature is evaluated independently with respect to the target variable, and features with higher test statistic values are considered more relevant. The application of this method to feature filtering in microarray data is documented in \cite{grisci2019}.

Since the test evaluates each feature separately, the method is univariate and does not account for potential dependencies or interactions between features. Kruskal Wallis test yields feature importance scores and is therefore classified as a weight-based feature selection approach.

\subsection{Mutual Information Filter}
Mutual Information, as introduced in Section XXXXX, is applied as a filter-based feature selection method. It allows measuring the relevance of each feature by quantifying how much information it provides about the target variable. Based on these values, features can be assigned importance weights. Similarly to other univariate filters, each feature is evaluated independently, without accounting for dependencies or interactions between variables.

\subsection{The Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance}
Minimal Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) \cite{peng2005} is a filter-based feature selection method that iteratively selects features by maximizing relevance to the target variable while minimizing redundancy with respect to the already selected subset. Relevance and redundancy are defined in Sections XXXXX and XXXXX, respectively. At each iteration, the method selects the feature $X^{(j)}$ that maximizes the mRMR criterion:
\begin{equation}
j^* = \arg\max_{j \in \{1,\dots,d\} \setminus S} \Bigl[ I\bigl(X^{(j)}; c\bigr) - R\bigl(X^{(j)}, S\bigr) \Bigr].
\end{equation}

Here, $\{1,\dots,d\}$ denotes the set of all feature indices, and $S \subset \{1,\dots,d\}$ is the set of already selected features. The term $I(X^{(j)}; c)$ measures the relevance of feature $X^{(j)}$ with respect to the class label $c$, while $R(X^{(j)}, S)$ quantifies its redundancy with respect to the selected subset.

The algorithm is terminated after selecting a predefined number $K$ of features. This greedy algorithm returns an ordered subset of features.

\subsection{ReliefF}
ReliefF \cite{kononenko1994relieff} is a filter-based feature selection algorithm designed to estimate the importance of features based on how well their values distinguish between instances of different classes. Unlike univariate methods, ReliefF is able to capture feature dependencies to some extent by considering the local neighborhood of each instance.

The algorithm works by repeatedly sampling an instance from the dataset and identifying its nearest neighbors: instances from the same class (nearest hits) and instances from different classes (nearest misses). For each attribute \(A\), ReliefF updates a weight according to how differently the attribute behaves between the sampled instance and its neighbors. Differences with nearest hits decrease the weight, while differences with nearest misses increase it. The contribution of misses is weighted by the class prior probabilities \(P(c)\), ensuring that their total influence is comparable to that of hits. A pseudocode description of the algorithm is provided in \cite{barbieri2022}, and is presented in Algorithm~\ref{alg:relieff}.


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{ReliefF Pseudo-code}
\label{alg:relieff}
\KwIn{$R$: set of all instances, $C$: set of classes, $k$: number of neighbors}
\KwOut{$W$: vector of feature weights}

$n \leftarrow |A|$\;
$W \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$ of size $|R|$\;

\For{$i = 1$ \KwTo $|R|$}{
    $H \leftarrow k\_nearest\_hits(k, R_i, R)$\;

    \ForEach{$c \in C \setminus class(R_i)$}{
        $M_c \leftarrow k\_nearest\_misses(c, k, R_i, R)$\;
    }

    \For{$a = 1$ \KwTo $|A|$}{
        \ForEach{$hit \in H$}{
            $W[a] \leftarrow W[a] - \frac{diff(R_i, hit)}{n \cdot k}$\;
        }

        \ForEach{$c \in C \setminus class(R_i)$}{
            \ForEach{$miss \in M_c$}{
                $d \leftarrow \frac{P(c)}{1 - P(class(R_i))} \cdot \frac{diff(R_i, miss)}{n \cdot k}$\;
                $W[a] \leftarrow W[a] + d$\;
            }
        }
    }
}

\Return $W$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Decision Tree}
A decision tree, is a predictive tree-based model model, constructed in a top-down, recursive manner. The structure consists of internal nodes, which represent decision rules based on specific features, and leaf nodes, which represent the final predicted outcomes. The tree is built by partitioning the data at each node using an impurity measure, such as gini coefficient\footnote{The Gini coefficient (or Gini impurity in the context of decision trees) is a measure of node impurity used in classification tasks. It is defined as \( G = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i^2 \), where \(p_i\) is the proportion of samples belonging to class \(i\) in a given node and \(K\) is the number of classes. A Gini value of 0 indicates a perfectly pure node (all samples belong to one class), while higher values indicate greater class heterogeneity. It is commonly used in algorithms such as Classification and Regression Trees (CART) to evaluate splits.} for classification or mean squared error (MSE) for regression. This process acts as an implicit form of feature selection, as the algorithm prioritizes features that yield the greatest reduction in impurity, thereby identifying the most discriminative variables in the dataset. In addition, feature importance scores can be extracted from the trained model based on the contribution of each feature to impurity reduction across all splits. One of the most widely used decision tree algorithms is Classification and Regression Trees (CART) \cite{breiman1984}.

\subsection{Random Forrest}
Random Forest \cite{breiman2001} is an ensemble learning method based on decision trees, used for both classification and regression tasks. It builds a collection of decision trees during training, where each tree is constructed on a bootstrap sample (i.e., random sampling of instances with replacement) and at each split considers only a random subset of features. The final prediction is obtained by aggregating the outputs of all trees, typically through majority voting for classification or averaging for regression. This randomization reduces overfitting compared to a single decision tree and improves generalization performance. Additionally, Random Forest provides feature importance measures based on the average contribution of each feature to impurity reduction across all trees, making it suitable for embedded feature selection.

\subsection{Support Vector Machines}
Support Vector Machines (SVM) \cite{cortes1995svm} are a class of supervised learning models used for classification and regression. The method seeks to find an optimal hyperplane in an n-dimensional space that separates data points of different classes with the maximum possible margin, i.e., maximizing the distance between the hyperplane and the closest observations from each class. When the data is not linearly separable, SVM can be extended using kernel functions and a soft-margin formulation, which allows for some misclassifications while maintaining good generalization.

The optimization problem is typically expressed as:
\[
\min_{w,b} \; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \max\left(0,\, 1 - y_i (w^\top x_i - b)\right) + \lambda \|w\|^2
\]

where \( w \) is the weight vector normal to the hyperplane, \( b \) is the bias (offset), \( n \) is the number of observations, \( x_i \) is the feature vector of the \( i \)-th observation, and \( y_i \in \{-1,1\} \) denotes its class label. The first term corresponds to the hinge loss, while the second term is an \( L_2 \) regularization penalty controlled by \( \lambda \). In embedded feature selection settings, SVM can provide feature importance by analyzing the learned weight vector in linear models, where features with larger absolute coefficients contribute more to the decision boundary.

\subsection{LassoNet}
While feature selection methods have been extensively studied in the context of linear models, particularly Lasso regression, identifying relevant features in neural networks remains a challenging problem. LassoNet \cite{lemhadri2021lassonet} addresses this challenge by extending Lasso-style sparsity to feedforward neural networks.

The key idea of LassoNet is to combine an $L_1$-penalized skip-layer (residual) connection from the input directly to the output with a hierarchical constraint, which enforces that a feature can influence the nonlinear component of the network only if its corresponding weight in the residual connection is non-zero. Residual architectures, popularized by Kaiming He, are known to be easier to train and exhibit strong approximation capabilities \cite{he2016resnet}. The general architecture of LassoNet is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:lassonet}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/lassonet.png}
    \caption{\textbf{LassoNet architecture}. It is composed of a single residual connection (highlighted in green) combined with an arbitrary feed-forward neural network (shown in black). The outputs of the residual connection and the first hidden layer are jointly processed through a hierarchical soft-thresholding optimization step.}
    \label{fig:lassonet}
\end{figure}

In this work, we apply LassoNet to supervised learning tasks, although it can also be extended to unsupervised tasks, as discussed in the literature. We consider data in a $d$-dimensional space, where $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denotes the covariate vector and $y$ the corresponding response variable. The model function is defined as:
\[
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}, W}(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{x} + g_W(\mathbf{x}),
\]
where $g_W(\mathbf{x})$ denotes a arbitrary feedforward neural network with parameters $W$, and $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the weights of the skip-layer connection. 

Let $n$ denote the number of training observations and $d$ the input dimension. The function $g_W$ is assumed to be fully connected, and we denote by $K$ the number of units in the first hidden layer. For notational convenience, let $W^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times K}$ represent the weights of the first hidden layer. The empirical loss function is denoted by $L(\boldsymbol{\theta}, W)$, viewed as a function of the model parameters.

The LassoNet objective is defined as
\begin{equation}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{W}} \; \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{W}) + \lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_1
\end{equation}
subject to hierarchical constraint:
\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_j\|_{\infty} \leq M |\boldsymbol{\theta}_j|, \quad j = 1, \dots, d,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{W})$ is the empirical loss function and $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_j$ denotes the weights associated with feature $j$ in the first hidden layer.

The constraint
\begin{equation}
|\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{jk}| \leq M |\boldsymbol{\theta}_j|, \quad k = 1, \dots, K,
\end{equation}
links the linear (skip-layer) and nonlinear components of the model. It enforces that the contribution of feature $j$ in the hidden layers is controlled by its corresponding coefficient $\boldsymbol{\theta}_j$. In particular, if $\boldsymbol{\theta}_j = 0$, then $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_j = \mathbf{0}$, and the feature is completely excluded from the network. Thus, sparsity in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, induced by the $\ell_1$-penalty, directly determines feature selection in the entire model.

The parameter $M$ controls the strength of this hierarchy. For $M = 0$, the nonlinear component vanishes and the model reduces to Lasso regression. As $M \to \infty$, the constraint becomes inactive and the model approaches a standard feedforward neural network.

Training proceeds by alternating between a gradient descent step on $(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{W})$ and a hierarchical proximal update applied to $(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{W}^{(1)})$. This makes the method easy to implement and computationally efficient, with the full regularization path obtainable at a cost comparable to training a single model.


Training proceeds by alternating between a gradient descent step on $(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{W})$ and a structured proximal update applied to $(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{W}^{(1)})$. A proximal operator is an optimization step that solves a local regularized minimization problem, typically combining a quadratic term (which keeps the solution close to the current estimate) and a penalty term that enforces structure such as sparsity. The proximal update is applied separately for each feature $j$, where the pair $(\theta_j, \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_j)$ is updated jointly as a small block optimization problem.

In LassoNet, the proximal step is defined, for each $j = 1, \dots, d$, as the solution of
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta_j \in \mathbb{R}, \; \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_j \in \mathbb{R}^K}
\; \frac{1}{2} (\tilde{\theta}_j - \theta_j)^2
+ \frac{1}{2} \|\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(1)}_j - \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_j\|_2^2
+ \lambda |\theta_j|
\end{equation}
subject to
\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_j\|_{\infty} \leq M |\theta_j|,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\theta}_j$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(1)}_j$ denote the intermediate values obtained after the gradient descent step. 

LassoNet produces a sequence of models along a regularization path indexed by the parameter $\lambda$, which controls the level of sparsity. Two strategies can be used to compute this path: dense-to-sparse and sparse-to-dense. In the dense-to-sparse approach, training starts with a small value of $\lambda$, where the model is dense, and $\lambda$ is gradually increased, leading to progressively sparser solutions. In contrast, the sparse-to-dense approach starts with a large $\lambda$, where most features are inactive, and gradually decreases it.

In practice, the dense-to-sparse path is preferred, as it tends to provide more stable solutions and better generalization performance. This is because the model begins in a well-trained dense regime and gradually removes less relevant features, rather than attempting to introduce them later.

Feature importance is derived from the evolution of parameters along the regularization path. Since the hierarchical constraint ensures that a feature is active in the network only if $\theta_j \neq 0$, feature selection is fully determined by the skip-layer weights. A common approach is to define the importance of feature $j$ as the largest value of $\lambda$ for which $\theta_j \neq 0$, i.e., the point along the path where the feature is removed from the model. Thus, features that remain active for larger values of $\lambda$ are considered more important. This provides a natural ranking of features, analogous to coefficient paths in Lasso regression.
