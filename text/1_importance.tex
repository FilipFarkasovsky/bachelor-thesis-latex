\section{Overview of Neural Networks}
\label{sec:overview-neural-networks}

Neural networks are one of the most popular algorithms of machine learning. They were loosely inspired by the structure and function of biological neurons, which are the fundamental units of nervous tissue. To distinguish them from their biological counterpart, sometimes the term \textit{Artificial neural networks (ANNs)} is used. 

Neural network can be viewed as a composition of affine transformations and non-linear activation functions, which allows them to serve as universal approximators. This property is established in many theorems; several of which are mentioned by Goodfellow in \cite{goodfellow2016}. Their ability to capture complex patterns makes them widely applicable in areas such as computer vision, natural language processing, and predictive modeling. A detailed treatment of neural network applications and methodologies can be found in the comprehensive review by Abiodun et al. \cite{abiodun2019}.

\subsection{Network architectures}
\label{sec:network-architectures}
The architecture of a neural network defines how these neurons are structured and how information flows through the system. Different architectures are designed to capture specific types of patterns in data, such as spatial structure, temporal dependencies, or general nonlinear relationships.

The architecture of a neural network describes how neurons and layers are organized, connected, and how they transform information.

\textbf{\\Neuron in an Artificial Neural Network\\} 
\emph{Neuron} is the fundamental computational unit of an Artificial Neural Network, abbreviated as ANN. It is defined as a parametric function that maps an input vector to a scalar output through a composition of a affine transformation and activation function. The structure of a single neuron is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:neuron}.

Let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the input vector, where $d$ is the number of input features. A neuron is parameterized by weights $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$, which are collectively referred to as learnable parameters and often denoted by $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\mathbf{w}, b)^{\top}$.

First, the input undergoes an affine transformation to produce the \emph{pre-activation value}:
\begin{equation}
z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = \sum_{i=1}^{d} w_i x_i + b,
\end{equation}
The scalar $z$ is then passed through a fixed, typically non-linear \emph{activation function} $\phi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, producing the \emph{activation value}:
\begin{equation}
a = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \phi(z)\end{equation}

A special case of an artificial neuron is the \emph{perceptron}, which acts as a linear binary classifier. It uses \emph{Heaveside} step activation function:

\begin{equation}
\phi(z) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } z \geq 0, \\
0 & \text{if } z < 0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

This defines a decision boundary given by:

\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{d} w_i x_i + b = 0.
\end{equation}

which corresponds to a hyperplane in $\mathbb{R}^d$, provided that the data are linearly separable.

\textbf{\\Multilayer Architectures} \\
The \emph{perceptron model} is limited to solving linearly separable problems. To overcome this limitation, neural networks are extended into multilayer architectures, where multiple neurons are organized into layers.
A network typically consists of an \emph{input layer} that receives data, \emph{hidden layers} that perform computations, and an \emph{output layer} that produces predictions (see Figure \ref{fig:neural-network}).


\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/tikz1.pdf}
    \caption{Scheme of the Neural Network}
    \label{fig:neural-network}
\end{figure}



Neurons in hidden layer work the same way as a perceptrons; however instead of receiving the input vector, they take the outputs of neurons from the previous layer. Because of this similarity those networks are referred to as \textit{multilayer perceptrons (MLPs)}, which are fully connected feedforward neural networks. The term \textit{feedforward network} refers to an architecture, where the information flows strictly in one direction - from the input layer to the output layer.

In artificial neural networks, it is common for all neurons in the hidden layers to share the same activation function, though this is not required; the choice of activation functions will be discussed in the subsections~\ref{subsec:activation-functions-hidden-layer} and \ref{subsec:activation-functions-ouput-layer}. Likewise, the hidden layers do not need to have the same number of neurons, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:convolutional-neural-network}.

The flow of signals in neural networks does not have to be strictly one-directional. In tasks involving sequential data, where the order of inputs matters, \textit{recurrent neural networks (RNNs)} are used, in which neurons are able to store information from earlier signal passes and take it into account during future activations (see Figure~\ref{fig:reccurent-neural-network}). This is achieved through feedback connections (loops), which allow information to persist over time. Such properties are essential for applications like time series analysis and speech recognition \cite{graves2013speech}.

Neural networks also do not need to be fully connected. Partially connected architectures are used in \textit{convolutional neural networks (CNNs)} \cite{oshea2015cnn} (see Figure~\ref{fig:convolutional-neural-network}), which are primarily applied to visual recognition tasks. These networks alternate between \textit{convolutional and pooling layers}: the input is broken into small patches, which are processed through repeated layers. Early layers detect simple patterns like edges, while later ones combine them into more complex patterns. Due to sparse connectivity, training is more efficient, and CNNs achieve strong performance in visual recognition tasks when properly designed \cite{lecun1998}.

The architecture of a neural network is therefore determined by several design choices, including the depth (number of hidden layers), their width (number of neurons per layer), the pattern of connectivity between neurons, the direction of information flow, the choice of layer types and activation functions, as well as other architectural components. Such choices are referred to as \emph{hyperparameters} of the network.

\section{Network notation}

In this section, we introduce notation for individual neurons and their weights.  As an example, consider a fully connected feedforward network described with $L$ layers, and denote its parameters as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $n^{(0)}, n^{(1)}, \dots, n^{(L)}$: number of neurons in each layer, where $d = n^{(0)}$ is the dimensionality of the input vector and $k = n^{(L)}$ is the number of neurons in the output layer,
    \item $\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(i)}$: weight vector of the $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer
    \item $b_{j}^{(i)}$: bias of the $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer,
    \item $z_{j}^{(i)}$: pre-activation value of the $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer, i.e., the input to the activation function,
    \item $a_{j}^{(i)}$: output of the $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer,
    \item $f^{(i)}$: activation function of neurons in the $i$-th layer; for simplicity, we assume it is the same for all neurons within a given layer.
\end{itemize}

The output of an arbitrary $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer $o_{i,j}$ can then be written as:
\begin{equation}
a^{(i)}_j = f^{(i)}\!\left( \langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_j, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_j \right).
\end{equation}

where we define the vector of activations of the $(i-1)$-th layer as
\[
\mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} = \big(a^{(i-1)}_1, a^{(i-1)}_2, \dots, a^{(i-1)}_{n^{(i-1)}}\big)^{\top}.
\]


From equation (2.3), we can rewrite the vector $\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$ as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(i)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
f^{(i)}\!\big( \langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_1, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_1 \big) \\
f^{(i)}\!\big( \langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_2, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_2 \big) \\
\vdots \\
f^{(i)}\!\big( \langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}}, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}} \big)
\end{pmatrix}
=
f^{(i)}\!\left(
\begin{array}{c}
\langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_1, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_1 \\
\langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_2, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_2 \\
\vdots \\
\langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}}, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}}
\end{array}
\right).
\end{equation}
where the function $f^{(i)}$ is applied element-wise.

The input vector can further be written as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = f^{(i)}\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(i)} \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(i)} \right),
\end{equation}

where
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{W}^{(i)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
(\mathbf{w}^{(i)}_1)^\top \\
(\mathbf{w}^{(i)}_2)^\top \\
\vdots \\
(\mathbf{w}^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}})^\top
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{b}^{(i)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
b^{(i)}_1 \\
b^{(i)}_2 \\
\vdots \\
b^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathbf{W}^{(i)}$ is a matrix whose rows are formed by the weight vectors of individual neurons in the $i$-th layer, and $\mathbf{b}_i$ is the vector of their biases arranged as a column vector.

Altogether, we obtain a concise recursive description of the output of the $i$-th layer of the neural network for $i \in \{2,3,\dots,L\}$, with the initial equation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = f^{(1)}\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(1)} \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)} \right),
\end{equation}
where the vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the input observations at the input layer. It should again be noted that the activation function $f_i$ is applied component-wise to a vector and does not represent a multivariate function in the usual sense.

We denote the whole neural network model as $f(\mathbf{x})$ or $f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$, where $\theta$ represents all trainable parameters of the network. The model can be viewed as a composition of layer-wise functions:
\begin{equation}
f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = f^{(L)} \circ f^{(L-1)} \circ \cdots \circ f^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}),
\end{equation}
where each layer is a mapping
$
f^{(i)} : \mathbb{R}^{n^{(i-1)}} \to \mathbb{R}^{n^{(i)}},
$
which transforms the output of layer $i-1$ into the input of layer $i$.

The final output of the network $\mathbf{a}^{(L)}$ represents the predicted value of the real-valued output variable $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^k$, where $k$ denotes the dimension of the output space (i.e., the number of output neurons). Thus, $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{a}^{(L)} = f(\mathbf{x})$.


In general, for an observation with index $i$, we may denote:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i = \mathbf{a}^{(L)}_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i).
\end{equation}

Evidently, in the special case $k = 1$, $\mathbf{y}_i$ reduces to a scalar $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$.

\subsection{Brief History of Neural Networks}
\label{sec:brief-notation}
Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb proposed that the connections (synapses) between neurons strengthen when they are activated together \cite{hebb1949}. This idea is often summarized by the phrase: “neurons that fire together, wire together”. \textit{Hebbian theory} provided a foundational idea, that networks could learn from experience.

In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt proposed the \textit{Perceptron} \cite{rosenblatt1958}, one of the first learning algorithms for neural networks. The perceptron was capable of supervised learning and classification tasks, which generated a lot of optimism about artificial neurons. 

However, this progress was later reduced by theoretical limitations identified by Minsky and Papert, who proved that single-layer linear networks could not solve problems such as the XOR function \cite{minsky1969}. This critique contributed to a decline in research activity of ANNs, often referred to as the “AI winter.” 

A key milestone in neural network research in the 1980s was the introduction of the \emph{backpropagation algorithm} \cite{rumelhart1986}, popularized by Geoffrey Hinton, David Rumelhart, and Ronald Williams. Backpropagation enabled efficient training of multi-layer neural networks by computing gradients of the \emph{loss function}. This algorithm helped to overcome the limitations of single-layer models \cite{lecun2015} and enabled the transition toward more complex architectures.

Another important factor was the increase in computational power, particularly the development of \emph{graphical processing units (GPUs)}, which enabled further advances in artificial neural networks.

During the 1990s, specialized neural network architectures were developed to address different types of tasks. \textit{Convolutional neural networks}, formalized by LeCun et al. \cite{lecun1998}, proved highly effective for image processing. \textit{Recurrent neural networks} were designed to model sequential data. These architectures enabled neural networks to tackle a broader range of problems, leading to significant improvements in vision, speech, and language applications \cite{lecun2015}.

\subsection{Activation Functions in the Hidden Layers}
\label{subsec:activation-functions-hidden-layer}
\emph{Activation functions} introduce non-linearity into neural networks, enabling them to approximate complex, non-linear functions. Without non-linear activation functions, a feedforward neural network reduces to a composition of affine transformations, which is itself an affine transformation, regardless of depth. Consequently, such a model would be equivalent to a single linear model.

A suitable activation function for hidden layers should satisfy the following properties:\begin{itemize}    
    \item \textbf{Differentiability:} The activation function should be differentiable almost everywhere to ensure that gradient-based optimization methods, such as \emph{backpropagation}, can be applied (discussed in section~\ref{subsec:backpropagation}). 
    
    \item \textbf{Computational efficiency:} The function should be computationally inexpensive to evaluate, as activation functions are applied repeatedly during both training and inference.
    
    \item \textbf{Gradient stability:} The function should mitigate \emph{vanishing} or \emph{exploding gradients}, ensuring stable signal propagation in deep architectures. These phenomena are discussed in section~\ref{subsec:vanishin-and-exploding-gradients}.
\end{itemize}


\textbf{\\Sigmoidal Functions\\}  
The \emph{sigmoid function} is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) := \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.
\end{equation}

However, for large values of $|z|$, the derivative $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr)$ approaches zero, which leads to the \emph{vanishing gradient problem} (see section~\ref{subsec:vanishin-and-exploding-gradients})~\cite{goodfellow2016}. Consequently, the sigmoid activation is rarely used in hidden layers of neural networks.

Another sigmoidal activation function, such as the \emph{hyperbolic tangent} ($\tanh(z) = 2\sigma(2z) - 1$), improve optimization dynamics by being zero-centered and reducing bias shift, yet they still suffer from vanishing gradients \cite{goodfellow2016}. 

\textbf{\\Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)\\}  
To mitigate these issues, the \emph{Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)} has become the standard choice for hidden layers. The ReLU function is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) := \max(0, z)
\end{equation}

It is widely used due to its simplicity and effectiveness. ReLU allows sparse activation, where only a subset of neurons are active at a time. It is computationally more efficient than sigmoidal functions, avoids saturation for positive inputs, and converges faster in deep architectures \cite{aggarwal2018}. 

A known drawback of the ReLU function is the so-called \emph{dying ReLU problem}~\cite{nair2010relu}. Consider a neuron with pre-activation $z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$. If during training $z \leq 0$ for all inputs in the data distribution, then the output is identically zero. Since the gradient of the ReLU function is also zero for $z < 0$, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dz} \max(0, z) = 0 \quad \text{for } z < 0,
\end{equation}
the corresponding parameters receive no gradient updates, effectively preventing further learning in that neuron.

To mitigate this, the \textit{Leaky ReLU} extension introduces a small positive slope $\alpha$ (typically $\alpha = 0.01$) for negative inputs:
\begin{equation}
\phi(z) := \max(\alpha z, z).
\end{equation}

This function addresses the dying ReLU problem by allowing a small, non-zero gradient for $z < 0$, and thus preventing the neurons from becoming permanently inactive during training.

\textbf{\\Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU)\\}
Another widely used alternative is the \textit{Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU)} \cite{hendrycks2016}, which weights inputs by their percentile under a standard normal cumulative distribution function $\Phi(z)$, such that $\phi(z) := z\Phi(z)$.

This provides a smooth, probabilistic alternative to piecewise linear activations. Empirically, GELU has been shown to improve performance in deep architectures, and thus became default choice in transformer-based models such as \textit{Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT)} \cite{radford2019} and \textit{Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)} \cite{devlin2018}.

\subsection{Activation Functions in Output Layer}
\label{subsec:activation-functions-ouput-layer}

The choice of activation function in the output layer of a neural network depends on the type of task and the desired output representation. For regression tasks, where the goal is to predict real-valued outputs, the identity function
\begin{equation}
\phi(z) := z
\end{equation}
is typically used.

For binary classification, the sigmoid function is commonly applied:
\begin{equation}
\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.
\end{equation}

Its output lies in the interval $(0,1)$, allowing interpretation as a conditional probability $P(y=1 \mid z)$ under a Bernoulli model.

For multiclass classification with $k$ classes, the softmax function is used. Given a vector of logits $\mathbf{z} = (z_1, z_2, \dots, z_k)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^k$, it is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{softmax}(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} e^{z_j}}, \quad i = 1, \dots, k.
\end{equation}

This mapping transforms $\mathbb{R}^k$ into the probability simplex. It satisfies two fundamental properties: positivity $\mathrm{softmax}(z_i) > 0$ and normalization $\sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathrm{softmax}(z_i) = 1$. Therefore, the output can be interpreted as a categorical probability distribution over $k$ classes.

In practice, softmax is applied to the output of the final linear transformation (logits). The dimensionality of this layer must equal $k$, since each component corresponds to one class probability.

\subsection{Loss Functions}
\label{sec:loss-functions}
A \emph{Loss function} quantifies the discrepancy between a model prediction $\hat{y}$ and the corresponding target value $y$. It serves two main purposes: (i) it provides a quantitative measure of model performance, and (ii) it defines the objective function used in gradient-based optimization methods.

Given a dataset of $n$ observations $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$, the \emph{empirical risk (average loss)} associated with a model parameterized by $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\bigl(y_i, f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i)\bigr).
\end{equation}

Here, $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i)$ denotes the model prediction for input $\mathbf{x}_i$, and $\ell(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a \emph{sample loss function} measuring the discrepancy between prediction and target.

The objective of model training is to find optimal parameters that minimize the empirical risk:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).
\end{equation}

The choice of loss function depends on the type of task. For regression tasks, the \textit{squared error loss} is commonly used:

\begin{equation}
    l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) = {\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2}
\end{equation}

Minimizing the squared error loss corresponds to the \emph{ordinary least squares (OLS)} under standard assumptions, i.e., a linear model with additive noise. This loss function penalizes larger errors more heavily due to the quadratic term, which makes it sensitive to outliers.

For binary classification, the \textit{binary cross-entropy} loss is used, which is the the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli distribution:

\begin{equation}
    l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) = - \Big[ y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - \hat{y}_i) \Big],
\end{equation}

Here, \(\hat{y}_i \in (0,1)\) represents the predicted probability of the positive class.

For multiclass classification with $k$ classes, the \textit{categorical cross-entropy loss} is used in combination with the softmax activation:

\begin{equation}
    l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) = - \sum_{j=1}^{k} y_{i,j} \log \hat{y}_{i,j},
\end{equation}

where the target vector is typically represented in one-hot encoding:

\begin{equation}
    y_{i,j} = 
    \begin{cases} 
        1 & \text{if sample $i$ belongs to class $j$,} \\
        0 & \text{otherwise,} 
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

and \(\hat{y}_{i,j}\) is the predicted probability for class $j$  of sample $i$ .

In classification tasks solved by neural networks, the model typically ouputs a vector of predicted probabilities obtained through the softmax or sigmoid activation function. To transform these probability scores into one class decision, a \emph{decision threshold}, or also called \textit{cut-off} point must be specified. In binary classification, a default threshold is 0.5, but by adjusting this value, we can better suit the model to the requirements of a specific application. In multinomial classification usually an observation is assigned with the class with highest predicted probability.

\subsection{Gradient Descent}
\label{sec:gradient-descent}
\emph{Gradient descent} is an optimization algorithm used to minimize a loss function in machine learning models, including neural networks. Its main idea is to iteratively update the model parameters in the direction of the negative gradient, which locally indicates the steepest decrease of the objective function. 

Formally, for a set of parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and a differentiable loss function $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, the update rule is given by:


\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_t),
\end{equation}

where $\eta >0$ is the \textit{learning rate} controlling the step size, and $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta_t})$ is the gradient of loss function with respect to all parameters at iteration $t$.

Under conditions such as convexity of $\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta_t}}$ and Lipshitz-continuity of its gradients, convergence to the global minimum is guaranteed. However, these conditions are rarely satisfied in practice; consequently the algorithm may converge to a local minimum, or saddle points, or exhibit oscillatory behavior depending on the choice of the learning rate.

In practice, different variants of gradient descent are used depending on how the gradient is estimated from data.

In \emph{batch gradient descent}, the gradient is computed over the entire dataset:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell\bigl(y_i, f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i)\bigr).
\end{equation}

This provides an exact estimate of the true gradient but can be computationally expensive for large datasets.

In \emph{stochastic gradient descent (SGD)}, the parameters are updated using a single randomly selected sample:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell\bigl(y_i, f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i)\bigr).
\end{equation}

This introduces stochastiity into the optimization process, which may help escape shallow local minima, but can lead to high variance in parameter updates.

\emph{Mini-batch gradient descent} combines both approaches by using a randomly sampled subset $B \subset \{1,\dots,n\}$ of size $m$, where $1 < m < n$:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i \in B} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell\bigl(y_i, f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i)\bigr).
\end{equation}

This approach is widely used in practice due to its computational efficiency and more stable convergence behavior compared to pure SGD \cite{gfg2025}.

\subsection{Backpropagation Algorithm}
\label{subsec:backpropagation}
A neural network can be represented as a composition of layer-wise functions:
\begin{equation}
    f(\mathbf{x}) = f^{(L)} \circ f^{(L-1)} \circ \cdots \circ f^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}

Each layer function is vector-valued, and thus its derivatives are understood in terms of Jacobian matrices. Applying the multivariate chain rule yields:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}
=
\frac{\partial f^{(L)}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial f^{(L-1)}}
\cdot
\frac{\partial f^{(L-1)}}{\partial f^{(L-2)}}
\cdots
\frac{\partial f^{(1)}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}.
\end{equation}

Consequently, the derivative of the network output with respect to the input is given by the product of these Jacobians.

Training is based on two alternating procedures: \emph{forward computation}, which produces predictions, and \emph{backpropagation}, which computes gradients needed to update parameters.

\textbf{\\Forward Computation\\}
\emph{Forward computation (forward pass)} refers to propagating input data through a feedforward neural network to obtain an output.

Consider a feedforward neural network with \( L \) layers. For layer \( i \), the computation is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}^{(i)} = \mathbf{W}^{(i)} \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(i)}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = \phi\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{(i)}\right)
\end{equation}

The final output is \( \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{a}^{(L)} \)  is compared to the target \( \mathbf{y} \) using a loss function \( \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \).

\textbf{\\Backpropagation\\}
\emph{Backpropagation} applies the chain rule of calculus to compute gradients efficiently by propagating \emph{error signals} from the output layer back to the input layer.

Define the \emph{error at layer \( l \)} as:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)}}
\end{equation}

For the final layer \( L \), the error is computed as:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} =
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{a}^{(L)}} \odot \phi'\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{(L)}\right)
\end{equation}

where \( \odot \) denotes element-wise multiplication.

For hidden layers, the error is propagated recursively:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} =
\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top} \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}
\odot \phi'\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right)
\end{equation}

Once \( \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} \) is known, gradients with respect to parameters are given by:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}} =
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} \left(\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(l)}} =
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}
\end{equation}

Backpropagation avoids redundant computation of intermediate derivatives by reusing previously computed error terms. As a result, the complexity of gradient computation is linear with respect to the number of parameters, $\mathcal{O}(|\boldsymbol{\theta}|)$ \cite{goodfellow2016}.

\subsection{Vanishing and Exploding Gradients}
\label{subsec:vanishin-and-exploding-gradients}
The \emph{vanishing gradient problem} occurs when gradients decrease exponentially as they are propagated backward through a deep neural network. As a result, parameter updates in earlier layers become undesirably small, effectively slowing or stopping learning in these layers.

This issue is particularly common when using activation functions such as the sigmoid or hyperbolic tangent. For the sigmoid function, the derivative is given by
\begin{equation}
\phi'(z) = \phi(z)\bigl(1 - \phi(z)\bigr).
\end{equation}

Sinc $\phi(z) \in (0,1)$, the derivative is bounded by $0 < \phi'(z) \leq 0.25$, with the maximum attained at $z = 0$. Repeated multiplication of such small values across many layers leads to gradients approaching zero.

The \textit{exploding gradient problem} occurs when gradients grow exponentially during backpropagation, typically due to weights being initialized with large values or derivatives consistently greater than one\cite{goodfellow2016}. While the \textit{ReLU} activation mitigates vanishing gradients by maintaining a unit derivative for positive inputs ($\phi'(z) = 1$ for $z > 0$), deep architectures remain sensitive to initialization. Poorly scaled weights can still trigger exploding gradients or, conversely, lead to \textit{dying neurons} if pre-activations consistently fall into the zero-gradient region.

To reduce the risk of vanishing and exploding gradients, appropriate \emph{weight initialization techniques} are commonly employed. The \emph{Xavier initialization}~\cite{glorot2010} scales the initial weights according to the number of input and output units in a layer, aiming to preserve the variance of activations across network layers. This approach is particularly suitable for symmetric activation functions such as sigmoid or hyperbolic tangent. For ReLU-based architectures, \emph{Kaiming initialization}~\cite{he2015} is more commonly used, as it adjusts the variance specifically for activation functions that suppress negative inputs. Proper initialization also improves training stability and accelerates convergence in deep neural networks \cite{glorot2010,he2015}.

\subsection{Learning Rate}

For effective learning, it is crucial to choose appropriate \emph{learning rate}. Learning rate controls magnitude of parameter updates in a gradient-descent. A small learning rate may lead to slow convergence, while a too large learning rate may cause divergence or oscillatory behavior. Thus, using a fixed learning rate can cause instability or excessively slow convergence during training. 

Several strategies have been proposed to address this issue. One of the simplest approaches is \textit{exponential decay}, defined as:

% n overview of gradient descent optimization algorithms
\begin{equation}
    \eta_t = \eta_0 e^{-\gamma t},
\end{equation}
where $\eta_0$ denotes the initial learning rate, $\gamma>0$ is the decay rate, and $t$ is an iteration index. 

However, exponential decay does not adapt to the geometry of the optimization landscape. More advanced methods, known as adaptive optimization algorithms, adjust the learning rate individually for each parameter based on the history of gradients. The most effective methods are so called \emph{adaptive algorithms}, such as \textit{Adam}, \textit{RMSProp}, and \textit{Adagrad}~\cite{ruder2016overview, duchi2011adaptive, kingma2015adam}. These methods typically use running estimates of first and/or second moments of the gradients to scale parameter updates, which improves performance in settings where different parameters exhibit gradients of varying magnitude~\cite{ruder2016overview}. 

 Among these methods, Adam is one of the most widely used optimizers due to its strong empirical performance, robustness across a wide range of architectures, and relatively low sensitivity to the initial learning rate choice~\cite{kingma2015adam}. A commonly used extension is AdamW, which often provides improved regularization and generalization performance in deep learning models~\cite{loshchilov2019}.

\section{Evaluation of Model Performance}
\label{sec:evaluation-model-performance}

In this section, we introduce commonly used metrics for evaluating the performance of machine learning models.

\subsection{Criteria for classification tasks}
\label{sec:criteria-classification-tasks}

In classification, the goal is to assign observations to predefined classes. Model performance is evaluated by comparing predicted labels with true labels.


In binary classification, each observation belongs to one of four categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{True Positive (TP)}: a positive sample correctly classified as positive.
    \item \textbf{True Negative (TN)}: a negative sample correctly classified as negative.
    \item \textbf{False Positive (FP)}: a negative sample incorrectly classified as positive, also called a \textit{Type I error}.
    \item \textbf{False Negative (FN)}: a positive sample incorrectly classified as negative, also called a \textit{ Type II error}.
\end{itemize}

These four outcomes form the basis for evaluation.

In the multinomial case, \emph{one-vs-rest approach} is commonly used. Each class, observations belonging to one particular class are treated as positive, while all remaining observations are treated as negative. This reduces the problem to a series of binary classifications, allowing the same evaluation criteria to be used.

The results are typicaly summarized using a \textit{confusion matrix}, as shown in \ref{tab:confusion_matrix}:


\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & \text{Predicted Positive} & \text{Predicted Negative} \\
\hline
\text{Actual Positive} 
    & \text{True Positive} 
    & \text{False Negative (Type II Error)} \\
\text{Actual Negative} 
    & \text{False Positive (Type I Error)} 
    & \text{True Negative}
\end{array}
\]
\caption{Confusion matrix.}
\label{tab:confusion_matrix}
\end{table}

From the confusion matrix, several performance measures can be derived: \\

\textbf{Accuracy} is defined as the proportion of correctly classified observations: 

\begin{equation}
    \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
\end{equation}

Although intuitive, accuracy can be misleading in the presence of imbalanced class distributions. For example, consider a disease with a low prevalence of $0.5\%$. A naive model that classifies every observation as negative achieves an accuracy of 99.5\%. Despite this high value, the model fails to identify any positive cases. This shows that accuracy alone might not not be sufficient for evaluation of the model. 

\textbf{Precision} measures the proportion of correctly predicted positive samples among all predicted positive samples:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Recall (Sensitivity)} is proportion of correctly identified positive samples out of all actual positives: 
\begin{equation}
    \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Specificity} measures the proportion of correctly identified negative samples out of all actual negatives:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}
\end{equation}

\textbf{F1 score} is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:
\begin{equation}
    F1 = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
\end{equation}


\textit{The true positive rate (TPR)} corresponds to recall. \textit{The false positive rate (FPR)} is defined as $1 - \textit{specificity}$ and quantifies the proportion of negative samples incorrectly classified as positive.

\paragraph{Curve-based Metrics \\}
The metrics above depend on a fixed \emph{classification threshold}, and they do not capture how the model behaves across all possible thresholds. To address this problem, so called \emph{curve-based methods} are used.

\textit{The Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC curve)} plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate for different threshold values. It describes the trade-off between correctly identifying positive samples and incorrectly classifying negative samples. Since the ROC curve reflects both TPR and FPR across all possible choices of the cut-off point \(p\), it represents a robust measure of the quality of binary classifiers. The most widely used scalar summary of the ROC curve is the \textit{Area Under the Curve (AUC)}, which corresponds to the total area lying below it. Clearly, \( \text{AUC} \in [0,1] \), and higher values indicate a better classifier.

In some applications, only a specific part of the \emph{ROC curve} is relevant. \emph{The AUC (pAUC)} measures the area under the curve over a restricted range of FPR values, typically chosen to reflect the region where the classifier is expected to operate in practice. For example, in applications where a very low false positive rate is required, one may compute the pAUC over the interval $\mathrm{FPR} \in [0, \alpha]$ for a small $\alpha$.

\emph{The Youden index} is another useful measure for threshold selection. It is defined as the sum of sensitivity and specificity minus one, and is often used to identify an optimal operating point.

\emph{The precision–recall (PR) curve} plots precision against recall across thresholds. It is particularly useful for imbalanced datasets, where ROC curves may present an overly optimistic view of performance.

\emph{The area under the PR curve (AUPR)} summarizes the PR curve into a single value and reflects the model’s ability to identify positive samples when they are rare.

\subsection{Criteria for Regression Tasks}
\label{sec:criteria-regression-tasks}
In regression tasks, the goal is to predict a continuous target variable. 

Let $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ denote a dataset of input-output pairs, where $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is the corresponding target value. The predicted value is denoted by $\hat{y}_i = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i)$, where $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is a model parameterized by $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.

The most commonly used regression metrics are defined as follows.

\textbf{Mean Squared Error (MSE):}
\begin{equation}
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2.
\end{equation}

MSE measures the average squared deviation and is widely used as a loss function due to its differentiability and strong penalization of large errors.

\textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):}
\begin{equation}
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}.
\end{equation}

RMSE is a monotonic transformation of MSE and is expressed in the same units as the target variable, which facilitates interpretation.

\textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):}
\begin{equation}
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|.
\end{equation}

MAE measures the average absolute deviation and is less sensitive to outliers compared to MSE. It is sometimes used as a loss function, although its \break non-differentiability at zero may complicate optimization.

\section{Information Theory}
\label{sec:information-theory}
\emph{Information theory} is a branch of applied mathematics focused on quantifying the amount of information contained in a signal or random variable. The main idea is that observing unlikely events provides more information than observing likely ones. This framework is useful for feature selection, since it allows measuring how informative a feature is with respect to a target variable.

\textbf{\\Self-information\\}
Let $X$ be a discrete random variable with support $\mathcal{X}$, and let $P(x)$ denote its probability mass function. The information content of an outcome $x \in \mathcal{X}$ is measured by \emph{self-information}~\cite{shannon1948}:
\begin{equation}
I(x) = -\log P(x).
\end{equation}

Here, $P(x)$ is the probability of observing the event $x$, and the logarithm is typically taken in base 2 or the natural logarithm, depending on the context.

This definition satisfies the following intuitive properties \cite{goodfellow2016}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item events with high probability carry low information content,
    \item events with probability one carry zero information,
    \item for independent random variables, the joint self-information is additive.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{\\Entropy\\}
Self-information describes the information content of a single outcome. To quantify the uncertainty of an entire probability distribution of a discrete random variable $X$, \emph{(Shannon) entropy}~\cite{shannon1948} is used. It is defined as the expectation of self-information under $P$:
\begin{equation}
H(X) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[-\log P(x)] = - \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x)\log P(x).
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathcal{X}$ denotes the support of $X$, and $P(x)$ is the probability of outcome $x$.

For continuous random variables, the analogous concept is called \emph{differential entropy}, where the summation is replaced by an integral over the probability density function.

Note: In practice, expressions of the form $0 \log 0$ may appear in entropy computations. In information theory, these terms are defined via the limit
\begin{equation}
\lim_{p \to 0^+} p \log p = 0,
\end{equation}
which ensures that entropy remains well-defined even when some outcomes have zero probability.

\textbf{\\Kullback-Leibler Divergence\\}
To compare two probability distributions $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ defined on the same support $\mathcal{X}$, the \emph{Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence}~\cite{kullback1951} is defined as
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} \left[ \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \right] = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} [\log P(x) - \log Q(x)].
\end{equation}

The KL divergence is well-defined provided that $Q(x) > 0$ for all $x \in \mathcal{X}$ such that $P(x) > 0$.

The distribution $P$ is typically interpreted as a reference (or data-generating) distribution, while $Q$ represents a model or approximation.

The KL divergence quantifies the discrepancy between $P$ and $Q$ in terms of the expected log-likelihood ratio under $P$. It is always non-negative and equals zero if and only if $P = Q$ almost everywhere. It is not symmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality, and therefore it is not a metric.

\textbf{\\Cross-entropy\\}
Closely related to KL divergence is the \emph{cross-entropy} \cite{cover2006}, defined as
\begin{equation}
H(P, Q) = - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} [\log Q(x)].
\end{equation}

Here, $P$ and $Q$ denote probability distributions defined on the same support $\mathcal{X}$, and the expectation is taken with respect to $P$, in the same manner as in the definition of the KL divergence.

Cross-entropy can be decomposed as:
\begin{equation}
H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q),
\end{equation}
where $H(P)$ denotes the entropy of the distribution $P$.

Since $H(P)$ does not depend on $Q$, minimizing cross-entropy with respect to $Q$ is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence.

In machine learning, cross-entropy corresponds to the negative log-likelihood of the model $Q$ under data generated from $P$, which explains its widespread use as a loss function.

\textbf{\\Mutual Information\\}
\emph{Mutual information}\cite{shannon1948,cover2006} quantifies the amount of information shared between two random variables $X$ and $Y$. It measures how much knowledge of one variable reduces uncertainty about the other. It is defined as
\begin{equation}
I(X; Y) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim P(x,y)} \left[ \log \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)} \right],
\end{equation}

where $P(x,y)$ is the joint probability distribution of $X$ and $Y$, and $P(x)$, $P(y)$ are the corresponding marginal distributions.

Mutual information is symmetric, non-negative, and equals zero if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent. In feature selection, it is commonly used to quantify the relevance of a feature with respect to a target variable.


\textbf{\\Redundancy\\}
Let $X^{(i)}$ be a discrete random variable representing the $i$-th feature, defined on a common probability space. Let $S \subset \{1,\dots,d\} \setminus \{i\}$ denote a subset of feature indices other than $i$.

Here, $d \in \mathbb{N}$ denotes the total number of features in the dataset. Each observation $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a realization of a $d$-dimensional random vector
\[
\mathbf{X} = (X^{(1)}, \dots, X^{(d)}),
\]
defined on the same probability space.

We define the \emph{redundancy}~\cite{peng2005} of feature $X^{(i)}$ with respect to the set $S$ as the average mutual information between $X^{(i)}$ and the variables indexed by $S$:
\begin{equation}
R(X^{(i)}, S) = \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{j \in S} I\bigl(X^{(i)}; X^{(j)}\bigr).
\end{equation}

Here, $I(X^{(i)}; X^{(j)})$ denotes the mutual information between two discrete random variables $X^{(i)}$ and $X^{(j)}$, defined via their joint probability mass function
$P_{i,j}(x,y),$ induced by the underlying joint distribution of $\mathbf{X}$.

This quantity measures, on average, the pairwise statistical dependence between $X^{(i)}$ and the features in $S$. A larger value of $R(X^{(i)}, S)$ indicates stronger average dependence and therefore a higher degree of information overlap with the set $S$.

In feature selection, this measure can be used as a heuristic indicator of redundancy, since features with high pairwise mutual information relative to already selected variables tend to provide less additional information about the data-generating process.



\section{Statistical Learning Theory}
\label{sec:generalization-neural-networks}

\subsection{The "No Free Lunch" Theorem}
The \emph{"No Free Lunch" (NFL) theorem}~\cite{wolpert1997} states that, when performance is averaged uniformly over all possible data-generating distributions, all learning algorithms achieve identical expected performance.

In the context of ANNs, the NFL theorem implies that no particular architecture or training algorithm is superior across all possible problems. The effectiveness of a model depends on its inductive bias, i.e., the set of assumptions that constrain the hypothesis space. Such biases are introduced through architectural choices (e.g., depth, connectivity structure), regularization techniques, and optimization procedures,  within which chosen model implicitly encodes assumptions about the underlying data.

\subsection{Hypothesis Space and Vapnik–Chervonenkis Dimension}
In statistical learning theory, the \emph{hypothesis space} is the set of all functions from which a learning algorithm selects when fitting a model to data. Formally, let $\mathcal{X}$ denote an input space and $\mathcal{Y}$ an output space. A hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$ is a set of measurable functions $h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$. For models parameterized by a vector $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^p$, such as neural networks, the hypothesis space is explicitly defined as
$\mathcal{H} = \{ f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \;:\; \boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^p \},$
where each hypothesis is denoted as $h \equiv f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$.

In supervised learning, given a training sample $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, the learning algorithm selects a hypothesis $\hat{h} \in \mathcal{H}$ by minimizing an empirical risk of the form
\[
\hat{h} = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(h(x_i), y_i\big),
\]
where $\ell : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a sample loss function.

In the context of artificial neural networks (ANNs), the hypothesis space is determined by the architecture of the network, including the number of layers, the number of neurons per layer, the type of activation functions, and the connectivity pattern. 

For a fixed architecture, the hypothesis space consists of all functions that can be represented by varying the network parameters (weights and biases). Thus, training an ANN can be interpreted as searching within a high-dimensional hypothesis space to find a function that minimizes a given loss function.

For deep neural networks, the hypothesis space is typically extremely large due to the composition of multiple nonlinear functions. This leads to high \emph{expressive power}, i.e., the ability to approximate a broad class of functions \cite{goodfellow2016}.  


As an illustrative example, consider the task of recognizing handwritten digits from the MNIST dataset, which contains $70,000$ grayscale images of digits $0$--$9$, and with image dimension of $28 \times 28$ \cite{lecun1998, mnistdataset}.  

A fully connected feed-forward neural network with one hidden layer of 128 neurons has:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 784 input units,
    \item 128 hidden units,
    \item 10 output units.
\end{itemize}

The total number of parameters is then given by:
\[
784 \cdot 128 + 128 + 128 \cdot 10 + 10 = 101{,}770.
\]

Each parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{101{,}770}$ defines a function $h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \in \mathcal{H}$, illustrating that even relatively simple architectures induce high-dimensional hypothesis spaces.

To formalize the notion of model capacity, one commonly used concept is the \emph{Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension}~\cite{vapnik1998}. The VC dimension of a hypothesis space \( \mathcal{H} \) is defined as the largest number of points that can be \emph{shattered} by \( \mathcal{H} \). A set of points is said to be \emph{shattered} if, for every possible labeling of these points, there exists a hypothesis in \( \mathcal{H} \) that correctly classifies them.

\subsection{Generalization in ANNs}

However, such a large hypothesis space can lead to poor generalization. \textit{Generalization} refers to the ability of a learning algorithm to perform well on previously unseen data drawn from the same underlying distribution as the training data. While a model is optimized using a finite dataset, the ultimate goal is to learn a function that approximates the true data-generating process rather than fitting the observed samples.

Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote the input space and $\mathcal{Y}$ the output space. The data-generating process is modeled as an unknown probability distribution $D$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$.

The \emph{generalization error} is defined as the expected loss with respect to $D$:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\big[\,\ell(f_\theta(x), y)\,\big],
\]
where $\ell(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a loss function, and $f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ denotes the model parameterized by $\theta$.

In practice, since the distribution $D$ is unknown, the generalization error cannot be computed exactly and is instead approximated using a finite sample drawn from $D$.

\textbf{\\Training Error and Test Error\\}
The \emph{training error} is the average loss computed on the training dataset. It reflects how well the model fits the data it was trained on.

The \emph{test error} is the average loss computed on a separate dataset not used during training. This dataset is assumed to be representative of the same underlying distribution $D$ but is not used in parameter estimation. Therefore, the test error serves as a practical empirical estimate of the generalization error defined above.

While the training error is directly optimised during learning, the test error serves as an estimator of the generalisation error. A small training error does not necessarily imply good generalisation, as the model may have memorised the training data rather than learned the underlying patterns.

\textbf{\\Noise\\}
\emph{Noise} refers to the random variability or errors present in the data that cannot be explained by the underlying functional relationship between inputs and outputs. Noise can arise from measurement errors, incomplete information, or inherent randomness in the data-generating process.

Noise limits the achievable performance of any model. Even an optimal model cannot perfectly predict outputs if the data contains irreducible noise. In practice, excessive fitting of noise leads to poor generalisation.

\textbf{\\Overfitting and underfitting\\}
When the model fits the training data too poorly it said to underfit. \textit{Underfitting} occurs when the chosen hypothesis is too simple relative to the data distribution; this prevents the model from capturing essential patterns and leads to high training error.

Conversely, \textit{overfitting} arises, when the model captures not only essential patterns, but also noise. This happens when the hypothesis is more complex than the data on which the model was trained. As a result, the model achieves low training error but high generalization error, approximated by the test error.

The \textit{generalisation gap} is defined as the difference between the training error and the test (generalization) error. It measures how well the model transfers learned patterns to unseen data.

A large generalization gap indicates overfitting, while a small gap suggests that the model generalizes well. In deep learning, monitoring the generalization gap is a common diagnostic tool.

\textbf{\\Bias-variance trade-off\\}
A central concept closely linked to overfitting and underfitting is the \emph{bias–variance trade-off}. Let 
\(\hat f(x)\) denote the estimator learned from data, and let \(f^\ast(x)\) be the true underlying regression function. The \emph{expected prediction error} at a fixed point \(x\) can be decomposed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x) - f^\ast(x))^2\big]
    =
    \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)] - f^\ast(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
    +
    \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}.
\end{equation}

The bias term quantifies the error due to restrictive assumptions in the model. A model with high bias fails to approximate complex patterns and tends to underfit.

The variance, which underlies the problem of overfitting, increases with model complexity. As complexity grows, bias decreases while variance rises. A larger number of parameters allows the model to capture more complex relationships between predictors and the response.

Neural networks, because of their high capacity and extremely large hypothesis space, can achieve low bias but often exhibit large variance, especially when the number of parameters is high relative to the sample size. 

\textbf{\\Validation\\}
To estimate generalization performance and tune model hyperparameters, the dataset is typically partitioned into three disjoint subsets:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Training set}: used to fit the model parameters,
    \item \emph{Validation set}: used to select models and tune hyperparameters,
    \item \emph{Test set}: used for final evaluation of generalization performance.
\end{itemize}

This separation is essential to obtain an unbiased estimate of the test error and to avoid information leakage from the evaluation data into the training process.

A typical workflow proceeds as follows. First, the dataset is split into training, validation, and test subsets. The model is then trained on the training set for a given choice of hyperparameters. Its performance is evaluated on the validation set, which is used to guide hyperparameter tuning and model selection. This process may be repeated multiple times for different configurations. After selecting the final model, its generalization performance is assessed once on the test set. 

\subsection{Regularisation Techniques}
Deep neural networks are highly expressive models with large hypothesis spaces, which makes them prone to overfitting. To improve generalisation performance, it is necessary to control model complexity and prevent the model from fitting noise in the training data. This is achieved through \emph{regularisation techniques}, which impose constraints on the learning process.

\textbf{\\Parameter norm penalties\\}
One common approach to address problem of overfitting is by adding a penalty term to a loss function, which effectively controls model complexity by discouraging large parameter values.

The general form of a regularized cost function is given by
\begin{equation}
J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \lambda \Omega(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\end{equation}

where $L(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the empirical error as defined in Section~XXXXX, $\Omega(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is a penalty term that discourages excessive model complexity by constraining the parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}  \in \mathbf{R}^p$, and $\lambda \geq 0$ is the regularization parameter that balances the fit to the training data against the simplicity of the model.

\textbf{\\$L_1$ Regularization (LASSO)\\}
\emph{Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)}~\cite{tibshirani1996} uses an $L_1$ penalty:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(\theta) = \|\theta\|_1 = \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\theta_j|.
\end{equation}

$L_1$ regularization promotes sparsity in the parameter vector, meaning that some coefficients are driven exactly to zero, which can also be interpreted as an implicit form of feature selection.

\textbf{\\$L_2$ Regularization (Ridge)\\}
In $L_2$ regularization (also known as \emph{Ridge} or \emph{Weight decay})~\cite{hoerl1970}, the penalty is given by the squared Euclidean norm of the parameter vector:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(\theta) = \|\theta\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{p} \theta_j^2.
\end{equation}

$L_2$ regularization discourages large parameter values by penalizing their magnitude quadratically. Unlike $L_1$ regularization, it does not enforce sparsity but instead tends to distribute weights more evenly across features.

\textbf{\\Elastic Net regularization\\}
\emph{Elastic Net}~\cite{zou2005} combines both $L_1$ and $L_2$ penalties. The regularization term is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(\theta) = \alpha \|\theta\|_1 + (1 - \alpha)\|\theta\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controls the trade-off between the $L_1$ and $L_2$ components - it promotes sparsity and feature selection through the $L_1$ component, while the $L_2$ component improves stability and encourages grouping of correlated features.

\textbf{\\Early stopping\\}
Early stopping~\cite{aggarwal2018} is a regularisation technique based on monitoring model performance on a validation set during training. Instead of training the model until convergence, the process is stopped when the validation error starts to increase, indicating the onset of overfitting.

In practice, the data are split into training and validation sets, the model is trained iteratively (e.g., via gradient descent), and its performance on the validation set is evaluated after each epoch; training is terminated once the validation error does not decrease for a predefined number of iterations, to which we refer as patience parameter.

Early stopping can be interpreted as implicit regularisation, since iterative optimisation tends to increase model complexity over time, and stopping early prevents the model from reaching overly complex solutions.

\textbf{\\Dropout\\}
Dropout~\cite{aggarwal2018} is a stochastic regularisation technique in which neurons are randomly removed during training. Formally, each neuron is independently retained with probability $p$ and set to zero with probability $1-p$. This encourages more robust representations. At test time, all neurons are used with appropriately scaled outputs. Dropout can be also viewed as an approximation to averaging predictions over many network architectures.

\textbf{\\Data Augmentation\\}
Data augmentation~\cite{goodfellow2016} increases the effective size of the training dataset by applying transformations to existing samples. In image tasks, this may include rotations, translations, or scaling. In other domains, appropriate transformations depend on the structure of the data.

By exposing the model to a wider variety of inputs, data augmentation reduces overfitting and improves generalisation without explicitly modifying the model or loss function.
