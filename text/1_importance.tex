\section{Overview of Neural Networks}
\label{sec:overview-neural-networks}

Neural networks are one of the most popular algorithms of machine learning. They were loosely inspired by the structure and function of biological neurons, which are the fundamental units of nervous tissue. To distinguish them from their biological counterpart, sometimes the term \textit{Artificial neural networks (ANNs)} is used. 

Neural network can be viewed as a composition of affine transformations and non-linear activation functions, which allows them to serve as universal approximators. This property is established in many theorems; several of which are mentioned by Goodfellow in \cite{goodfellow2016}. Their ability to capture complex patterns makes them widely applicable in areas such as computer vision, natural language processing, and predictive modeling. A detailed treatment of neural network applications and methodologies can be found in the comprehensive review by Abiodun et al. \cite{abiodun2019}.

\subsection{Network architectures}
\label{sec:network-architectures}
The architecture of a neural network defines how these neurons are structured and how information flows through the system. Different architectures are designed to capture specific types of patterns in data, such as spatial structure, temporal dependencies, or general nonlinear relationships.

The architecture of a neural network describes how neurons and layers are organized, connected, and how they transform information.

\textbf{\\Neuron in an Artificial Neural Network\\} 
\emph{Neuron} is the fundamental computational unit of an Artificial Neural Network (ANN). It is defined as a parametric function that maps an input vector to a scalar output through a composition of a affine transformation and activation function. The structure of a single neuron is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:neuron}.

Let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the input vector, where $d$ is the number of input features. A neuron is parameterized by weights $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$, which are collectively referred to as learnable parameters and often denoted by $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\mathbf{w}, b)^{\top}$.

First, the input undergoes an affine transformation to produce the \emph{pre-activation value}:
\begin{equation}
z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = \sum_{i=1}^{d} w_i x_i + b,
\end{equation}
The scalar $z$ is then passed through a fixed, typically non-linear \emph{activation function} $\phi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, producing the \emph{activation value}:
\begin{equation}
a = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \phi(z)\end{equation}

A special case of an artificial neuron is the \emph{perceptron}, which acts as a linear binary classifier. It uses \emph{Heaveside} step activation function:

\begin{equation}
\phi(z) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } z \geq 0, \\
0 & \text{if } z < 0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

This defines a decision boundary given by:

\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b = 0.
\end{equation}

which corresponds to a hyperplane in $\mathbb{R}^n$, provided that the data are linearly separable.

\textbf{\\Multilayer Architectures} \\
The \emph{perceptron model} is limited to solving linearly separable problems. To overcome this limitation, neural networks are extended into multilayer architectures, where multiple neurons are organized into layers.
A network typically consists of an \emph{input layer} that receives data, \emph{hidden layers} that perform computations, and an \emph{output layer} that produces predictions (see Figure \ref{fig:neural-network}).


\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/tikz1.pdf}
    \caption{Scheme of the Neural Network}
    \label{fig:neural-network}
\end{figure}



Neurons in hidden layer work the same way as a perceptrons, except that instead of receiving the input vector, they take the outputs of neurons from the previous layer. Because of this similarity those networks are also called \textit{multilayer perceptron}, which are fully connected feedforward neural networks. \textit{Feedforward network} refer to the architecture, where the information flows strictly in one direction - from input layer to output layer. In ANNs is common for all neurons in the hidden layers to share the same activation function, though this is not required; the choice of activation functions will be discussed in the \textcolor{green}{Subsection XXX}. Likewise, the hidden layers do not need to have the same number of neurons, as it is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:convolutional-neural-network}.



The flow of signals in networks does not have to be strictly one-directional. In some cases, we want to process a sequence of inputs with respect to their order. For such sequential data \textit{recurrent networks} are used, in which neurons are able to store information from earlier signal passes and take it into account during future activations (see Figure~\ref{fig:reccurent-neural-network}).. It is implemented by creating loops between neurons to allow information to persist across time steps. These properties are essential for tasks such as time series modeling or human speech recognition.

    \caption{Scheme of the Recurrent Neural Network}
    \label{fig:reccurent-neural-network}
\end{figure}

It is also not necessary for neural networks to use a fully connected architecture. A partially connected structure is used in \textit{convolutional networks} (see Figure~\ref{fig:convolutional-neural-network}), mainly applied to image and character recognition. Their idea is to alternate \textit{convolutional and pooling layers}: the input is broken into small patches, which are processed through repeated layers. Early layers detect simple patterns like edges, while later ones combine them into more complex shapes. Because the connections are sparse, training is faster, and with the right design, convolutional networks can achieve very strong performance in image recognition \cite{lecun2015}.

The architecture of a network is therefore determined by the number of hidden layers, their width (the number of neurons in each layer), the way the neurons are connected, and the manner in which the signal flows.

\section{Network notation}

In this section, we introduce notation for individual neurons and their weights.  As an example, consider a fully connected feedforward network described with $L$ layers, and denote its parameters as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $n^{(0)}, n^{(1)}, \dots, n^{(L)}$: number of neurons in each layer, where $d = n^{(0)}$ is the dimensionality of the input vector and $k = n^{(L)}$ is the number of neurons in the output layer,
    \item $\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(i)}$: weight vector of the $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer
    \item $b_{j}^{(i)}$: bias of the $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer,
    \item $z_{j}^{(i)}$: pre-activation value of the $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer, i.e., the input to the activation function,
    \item $a_{j}^{(i)}$: output of the $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer,
    \item $f^{(i)}$: activation function of neurons in the $i$-th layer; for simplicity, we assume it is the same for all neurons within a given layer.
\end{itemize}

The output of an arbitrary $j$-th neuron in the $i$-th layer $o_{i,j}$ can then be written as:
\begin{equation}
a^{(i)}_j = f^{(i)}\!\left( \langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_j, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_j \right).
\end{equation}

where we define the vector of activations of the $(i-1)$-th layer as
\[
\mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} = \big(a^{(i-1)}_1, a^{(i-1)}_2, \dots, a^{(i-1)}_{n^{(i-1)}}\big)^{\top}.
\]


From equation (2.3), we can rewrite the vector $\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$ as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(i)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
f^{(i)}\!\big( \langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_1, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_1 \big) \\
f^{(i)}\!\big( \langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_2, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_2 \big) \\
\vdots \\
f^{(i)}\!\big( \langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}}, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}} \big)
\end{pmatrix}
=
f^{(i)}\!\left(
\begin{array}{c}
\langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_1, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_1 \\
\langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_2, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_2 \\
\vdots \\
\langle \mathbf{w}^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}}, \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} \rangle + b^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}}
\end{array}
\right).
\end{equation}
where the function $f_i$ is applied element-wise.

The input vector can further be written as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = f^{(i)}\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(i)} \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(i)} \right),
\end{equation}

where
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{W}^{(i)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
(\mathbf{w}^{(i)}_1)^\top \\
(\mathbf{w}^{(i)}_2)^\top \\
\vdots \\
(\mathbf{w}^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}})^\top
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{b}^{(i)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
b^{(i)}_1 \\
b^{(i)}_2 \\
\vdots \\
b^{(i)}_{n^{(i)}}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathbf{W}^{(i)}$ is a matrix whose rows are formed by the weight vectors of individual neurons in the $i$-th layer, and $\mathbf{b}_i$ is the vector of their biases arranged as a column vector.

Altogether, we obtain a concise recursive description of the output of the $i$-th layer of the neural network for $i \in \{2,3,\dots,L\}$, with the initial equation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = f^{(1)}\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(1)} \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)} \right),
\end{equation}
where the vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the input observations at the input layer. It should again be noted that the activation function $f_i$ is applied component-wise to a vector and does not represent a multivariate function in the usual sense.

We denote the whole neural network model as $f(\mathbf{x})$ or $f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$, where $\theta$ represents all trainable parameters of the network. The model can be viewed as a composition of layer-wise functions:
\begin{equation}
f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = f^{(L)} \circ f^{(L-1)} \circ \cdots \circ f^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}),
\end{equation}
where each layer is a mapping
$
f^{(i)} : \mathbb{R}^{n^{(i-1)}} \to \mathbb{R}^{n^{(i)}},
$
which transforms the output of layer $i-1$ into the input of layer $i$.

The final output of the network $\mathbf{a}^{(L)}$ represents the predicted value of the real-valued output variable $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^k$, where $k$ denotes the dimension of the output space (i.e., the number of output neurons). Thus, $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{a}^{(L)} = f(\mathbf{x})$.


In general, for an observation with index $i$, we may denote:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i = \mathbf{a}^{(L)}_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i).
\end{equation}

Evidently, in the special case $k = 1$, $\mathbf{y}_i$ reduces to a scalar $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$.

\subsection{Brief History of Neural Networks}
\label{sec:brief-notation}
\textbf{Origins:} In 1949, Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb proposed that the connections (synapeses) between neurons strengthen when they are activated together \cite{hebb1949}. This is often summarized by the phrase attributed to him: “neurons that fire together, wire together”. So called \textit{Hebbian theory} provided a foundational idea, that networks could learn from experience.

\textbf{Early models:} A year later, the \textit{Perceptron}, introduced by Frank Rosenblatt \cite{rosenblatt1958}, was the first type of neural network designed to learn from examples. It combined the concept of a computational neuron with a learning algorithm that automatically adjusted its weights to separate inputs into classes using a linear decision boundary. The Perceptron was a single-layer neural network that applied a Heaviside (binary step) activation function to an affine combination of the inputs, producing a binary output. As a linear classifier, its decision boundary is a hyperplane in the input space, which means it can only correctly classify linearly separable data. Though groundbreaking, this linearity limited its ability to solve more complex classification problems.

\textbf{Setback:} The work of Minsky and Papert in \cite{minsky1969} demonstrated that single-layer linear networks could not solve problems such as the XOR function, which led to a temporary decline in research of neural networks \cite{lecture1_2017}. \textcolor{orange}{ Reference to paragraph in this thesis discussing limitations of perceptron capturing logic of XOR function in this thesis (maybe in similar way as it is showed by Goodfellow)}
\textcolor{pink}{TODO: Plot the graph in latex}

\textbf{Revival:} Neural networks regained attention with the development of multi-layer networks trained using backpropagation \cite{rumelhart1986}, \textcolor{green}{which will be discussed in Section X}.. This allowed networks to learn more complex patterns and revived research in deep learning.

\textbf{Specialised networks:} In the 1990s researchers introduced specialized architectures for different types of data. The development of \textit{convolutional neural networks}, as presented by LeCun, Bottou, Bengio, and Haffner in \cite{lecun1998}, for image processing, together with the use of \textit{recurrent neural networks} for sequential data, enabled neural networks to tackle a wider variety of problems. This led to strong improvements in vision, speech, and language applications \cite{lecun2015}.


\textbf{Developments up to 2026:} Breakthroughs such as AlexNet \cite{krizhevsky2012} and the \textit{transformer} architecture - built on the attention mechanism \cite{vaswani2017} - accelerated progress in image recognition and natural language processing. \textit{The attention mechanism} allows a neural network to focus on the most relevant parts of the input when making a prediction. Instead of treating all inputs equally, the model computes attention weights that measure how important each element (for example, each word in a sentence) is for the current step. This makes the model better at capturing long-range relationships and understanding context efficiently.

\subsection{Activation Functions in the Hidden Layers}
\label{sec:activation-functions-hidden-layer}
\emph{Activation functions} introduce non-linearity into model to learn complex data patterns. Without non-linear activation functions, a neural network would reduce to a linear model regardless of its depth, limiting its ability to approximate complex functions.

A suitable activation function for hidden layers should satisfy several key properties that ensure effective learning and stable optimization:
\begin{itemize}    
    \item \textbf{Differentiability:} The activation function should be differentiable (at least almost everywhere) to ensure that gradient-based optimization methods, such as backpropagation, can be applied effectively for parameter estimation.
    
    \item \textbf{Computational efficiency:} The function should be computationally inexpensive to evaluate, as activation functions are applied repeatedly during both training and inference, particularly in large-scale neural networks.
    
    \item \textbf{Gradient stability:} The activation function should support stable gradient propagation, reducing the risk of vanishing or exploding gradients, \textcolor{green}{which will be discussed in Section X} and thereby facilitating efficient and reliable training of deep architectures.
\end{itemize}

In this chapter, several of the most commonly used activation functions are introduced and discussed, with a focus on their properties and role in hidden layers of neural networks.



\textbf{\\Sigmoid Function\\}  
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
\end{equation}

The \emph{sigmoid function} maps inputs to the interval $(0, 1)$. While historically popular, it is less frequently used in hidden layers due to the \emph{vanishing gradient problem} (see Chapter XXXX) for large positive or negative inputs \cite{goodfellow2016}. 

\textbf{\\Hyperbolic Tangent(Tanh)\\}  
\emph{Hyperbolic tangent} is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) = \tanh(z) =\frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}}
= \frac{1 - e^{-2z}}{1 + e^{-2z}}
= 2\sigma(2z) - 1
\end{equation}

This function maps inputs to the interval $(-1, 1)$ and is zero-centered, which can improve convergence compared to the sigmoid function. However, it still suffers from vanishing gradients for very large positive or negative input values \cite{goodfellow2016}. 

\textbf{\\Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)\\}  
The ReLU function is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) = \max(0, z)
\end{equation}

It is widely used due to its simplicity and effectiveness. ReLU allows sparse activation, where only a subset of neurons are active at a time. It is computationally more efficient than sigmoidal functions, avoids saturation for positive inputs, and converges faster in deep architectures \cite{aggarwal2018}. 

Main problem with ReLU as activation function is that in learning process it is prone to creating so-called \textit{dead neurons}. This is a phenomenon, when its pre-activation value $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$ consistently falls into the non-positive region, causing its output to remain zero for given inputs during a training process. Since their gradient is always zero, their weights stop updating and they no longer contribute to learning.


\textbf{\\Leaky ReLU\\}
To cope with such issue, several variants have been proposed. One common approach is the \textit{Leaky ReLU}, which introduces a small slope for negative inputs:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) =
    \begin{cases}
        z & \text{if } z > 0 \\
        \alpha z & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is a small constant (e.g., $0.01$). This function addresses the ``dying ReLU'' problem by allowing a small, non-zero gradient for negative inputs.

\textbf{\\Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU)\\}
Another widely used alternative is the \textit{Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU)}. Its exact formulation is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) = z \cdot \Phi(z)
\end{equation}
where \( \Phi(z) \) denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. In practice, an efficient approximation is often used \cite{hendrycks2016}:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) \approx 0.5 z \left( 1 + \tanh\left( \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left( z + 0.044715 z^3 \right) \right) \right)
\end{equation}

Hendrycks and Gimpel in \cite{hendrycks2016} showed that its smooth, probabilistic, Gaussian-based formulation improves performance across a variety of tasks. GELU has become a standard choice in transformer-based architectures, including models such as 
\textit{Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT)} \cite{radford2019} and \textit{Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)} \cite{devlin2018}.

\subsection{Activation Functions in Output Layer}
\label{sec:activation-functions-ouput-layer}

The choice of activation function in the output layer of a neural network depends on the type of task and the desired output representation. For regression tasks, where the network predicts continous values, the \textit{identity function} $\phi(x) = x$ is commonly used. 

For binary classification, the sigmoid function is frequently used. Its output lies in the interval $(0,1)$, which can be interpreted as the probability of belonging to a positive class. Its simple derivative enables efficient gradient-based learning.

For multiclass classification, the \textit{softmax function} is commonly used. Given an input vector 
\(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^k\), softmax function can be defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{softmax}(x_i) = \frac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} e^{x_j}}, \quad i = 1,2, \dots, k.
\end{equation}

This transformation has two key properties. Positivity follows from the exponential function, ensuring that $\text{softmax}(x_i) > 0 \ \forall i$. Normalization is guaranteed by the denominator, which ensures that $\sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{softmax}(x_i) = 1$. Together, these properties ensure that the softmax output forms a valid probability distribution over the $k$ classes. Each component can therefore be interpreted as the model’s estimated probability that the input belongs to the corresponding class. 

Note that for this transformation to work, the last hidden layer must have the same number of neurons as the output layer.

\subsection{Loss Functions}
\label{sec:loss-functions}
\emph{Loss function} quantifies the difference between a neural network's prediction $\hat{y}$ and target value $y$. It can be used to both evaluate performance of the model, and for guidence in gradient-based learning, as it will be illustrated in the following subsection.

Given a dataset of $n$ observations, the \emph{empirical risk (average loss)} is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, \hat{y}_i)
\end{equation}

The objective of model estimation is to find parameters that minimize this empirical risk:

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \arg \min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} l\big(y_i, f(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta})\big)
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{x}_i$ denotes the input features, $f(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the model prediction, $\theta$ represents the model parameters, and $l(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the sample loss function.

The choice of loss function depends on the type of task. For regression tasks, the \textit{squared error loss} is commonly used:

\begin{equation}
    l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) = {\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2}
\end{equation}

Minimizing the squared error leads to the \emph{ordinary least squares (OLS)} estimator in linear regression. This loss function penalizes larger errors more heavily due to the quadratic term, which makes it sensitive to outliers.

For binary classification, the \textit{binary cross-entropy} loss is used, which is the the negative log-likelihood of Bernoulli distribution:

\begin{equation}
    l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) = - \Big[ y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - \hat{y}_i) \Big],
\end{equation}

where \(\hat{y}_i\) represents the predicted probability of the positive class.

For multiclass classification, the \textit{categorical cross-entropy loss} is used in combination with the softmax activation:

\begin{equation}
    l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) = - \sum_{k=1}^{k} y_{i,k} \log \hat{y}_{i,k},
\end{equation}

where 

\begin{equation}
    y_{i,k} = 
    \begin{cases} 
        1 & \text{if sample $i$ belongs to class $k$,} \\
        0 & \text{otherwise,} 
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

and \(\hat{y}_{i,k}\) is the predicted probability for class $k$  of sample $i$ .

In classification tasks solved by neural networks, the model typically ouputs a vector of predicted probabilities obtained through the softmax or sigmoid activation function. To transform these probability scores into one class decision, a \emph{decision threshold}, or also called \textit{cut-off} point must be specified. In binary classification, a default threshold is 0.5, but by adjusting this value, we can better suit the model to the requirements of a specific application. In multinomial classification usually an observation is assigned with the class with highest predicted probability.

\subsection{Gradient Descent}
\label{sec:gradient-descent}
\emph{Gradient descent} is an optimization algorithm used to train neural networks by minimizing loss function. Its main idea is iteratively adjusting the parameters of the model in the the direction that reduces loss the most. Formally, for a set of parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and a differentiable loss function $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, the update rule naturally is:


\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_t),
\end{equation}

where $\eta \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is a hyperparameter called \textit{learning rate}, which controls step size, and $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta_t})$ is the gradient of loss function with respect to all parameters at iteration $t$.

Under conditions such as convexity and Lipshitz-continuity of gradients, convergence to the global minimum is guaranteed. However, these conditions are rarely satisfied in practice; consequently the algorithm may converge to a local minimum, diverge or oscillate.

There are multiple ways in which the parameters can be updated at each step, depending on the choice of \textit{batch} - subset of training data used to estimate the gradient. In \textit{batch gradient descent}, the gradient is computed over the entire dataset:

\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) 
= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \, l\!\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i\right)
\end{equation}



where \(n\) is the total number of training samples and \( l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) \) is the loss for a single sample. This provides an accurate update but can be computationally expensive for large datasets.


In \textit{stochastic gradient descent (SGD)}, at each iteration the parameters are updated using a single randomly selected training sample:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
= \boldsymbol{\theta}_t
- \eta \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \, l\!\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i\right),
\end{equation}

which introduces stochasticity, potentially helping the optimizer escape shallow local minima, but may lead to unstable convergence.

\textit{Mini-batch gradient descent} updates the parameters using a randomly selected subset 
\( B \subset \{1, \dots, n\} \) of size \( m \), where \( 1 < m < n \).


\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
= \boldsymbol{\theta}_t
- \eta \, \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i \in B}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \, l\!\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i\right),
\end{equation}


This approach is the most commonly used in practice, because of its computational efficiency and smoother convergence \cite{gfg2025}.

\subsection{Backpropagation Algorithm}
A neural network can be represented as a composition of layer functions:
\begin{equation}
    f(\mathbf{x}) = f^{(L)} \circ f^{(L-1)} \circ \cdots \circ f^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}

Since these functions are vector-valued, derivatives are understood in the multivariate sense, i.e., as \emph{Jacobian matrices}. Applying the multivariate chain rule yields:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}
=
\frac{\partial f^{(L)}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial f^{(L-1)}}
\cdot
\frac{\partial f^{(L-1)}}{\partial f^{(L-2)}}
\cdots
\frac{\partial f^{(1)}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}.
\end{equation}

Consequently, the derivative of the network output with respect to the input is given by the product of these Jacobians.

Training is based on two alternating procedures: \emph{forward computation}, which produces predictions, and \emph{backpropagation}, which computes gradients needed to update parameters.

\textbf{\\Forward Computation\\}
\emph{Forward computation (forward pass)} refers to propagating input data through a feedforward neural network to obtain an output.

Consider a feedforward neural network with \( L \) layers. For layer \( i \), the computation is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}^{(i)} = \mathbf{W}^{(i)} \mathbf{a}^{(i-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(i)}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = \phi\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{(i)}\right)
\end{equation}

The final output is \( \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{a}^{(L)} \)  is compared to the target \( \mathbf{y} \) using a loss function \( \mathcal{L}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}, \mathbf{y}) \), such as \emph{mean squared error} or \emph{cross-entropy}.

\textbf{\\Backpropagation\\}
\emph{Backpropagation} applies the chain rule of calculus to compute gradients efficiently by propagating \emph{error signals} from the output layer back to the input layer.

Define the \emph{error at layer \( l \)} as:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)}}
\end{equation}

For the final layer \( L \), the error is computed as:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} =
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{a}^{(L)}} \odot \phi'\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{(L)}\right)
\end{equation}

where \( \odot \) denotes element-wise multiplication.

For hidden layers, the error is propagated backward as:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} =
\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top} \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}
\odot \phi'\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right)
\end{equation}

This recursive relationship allows gradients to be computed layer by layer starting from the output.

Once \( \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} \) is known, gradients with respect to parameters are given by:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}} =
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} \left(\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(l)}} =
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}
\end{equation}

These expressions are computationally efficient and \emph{backpropagation} thus reduces the computational complexity of gradient computation from exponential to linear in the number of layers by reusing intermediate derivatives through the chain rule \cite{goodfellow2016}.

\subsection{Vanishing and Exploding Gradients}
The \emph{vanishing gradient problem} occurs when gradients become extremely small as they are propagated backward through layers. As a result, the weights in earlier layers are updated very slowly or not at all.

This issue is particularly common when using activation functions such as the sigmoid or hyperbolic tangent. For example, the derivative of the sigmoid function is given by:

\begin{equation}
\phi'(z) = \phi(z)\left(1 - \phi(z)\right)
\end{equation}

Since \(\phi(z) \in (0,1)\), the derivative satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    \phi'(z) < 0.25
\end{equation}

Repeated multiplication of such small values across many layers leads to gradients approaching zero.

The exploding gradient problem arises when gradients grow exponentially during backpropagation. This typically happens when derivatives are consistently greater than 1, or when weights are poorly initialized.

Activation functions play a central role in these issues. In particular, activation functions such as ReLU have derivatives equal to 1 for positive inputs, which helps maintain gradient magnitude across layers. However, improper weight initialization can still lead to exploding gradients.

\subsection{Learning Rate}

For effective learning, it is crucial to choose appropriate \emph{learning rate}. Learning rate controls magnitude of each step in a \emph{gradient-descent}. A small learning rate can cause slow convergence. Conversely, a too large learning rate can cause divergence, or oscillatory behavior. Thus, selecting a fixed learning rate can cause instability or excessively slow convergence during training. Several modifications of learning rate are proposed. One of the most simple  is an \textit{exponential decay}:

% n overview of gradient descent optimization algorithms
\begin{equation}
    \eta_t = \eta_0 e^{-\gamma t},
\end{equation}
where $\eta_0$ denotes the initial learning rate, $\gamma>0$ is the decay coefficient, and $t$ is an iteration index. 

Exponential decay has some limitations, as it reduces the learning rate uniformly without considering the specific behavior of individual parameters. The most effective method are so called \emph{adaptive algorithms}, such as \textit{Adam}, \textit{RMSProp}, and \textit{Adagrad}~\cite{ruder2016overview, duchi2011adaptive, kingma2015adam}. \textcolor{pink}{GLOSSARY} These methods automatically adjust the step size for each parameter based on the history of gradients - this makes them useful when the gradients of some parameters are steep while others are shallow ~\cite{ruder2016overview}. Among them, Adam has become the most widely used optimizer due to its strong empirical performance, robustness across a variety of architectures, and relatively low sensitivity to the choice of the initial learning rate~\cite{kingma2015adam}.

\section{Evaluation of Model Performance}
\label{sec:evaluation-model-performance}

In this section, we introduce commonly used metrics for evaluating the performance of machine learning models.

\subsection{Criterias for classification tasks}
\label{sec:criteria-cassification-tasks}

In classification, the goal is to assign observations to predefined classes. Model performance is evaluated by comparing predicted labels with true labels.

In binary classification, each observation belongs to one of four categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{True Positive (TP)}: a positive sample correctly classified as positive.
    \item \textbf{True Negative (TN)}: a negative sample correctly classified as negative.
    \item \textbf{False Positive (FP)}: a negative sample incorrectly classified as positive, also called a \textit{Type I error}.
    \item \textbf{False Negative (FN)}: a positive sample incorrectly classified as negative, also called a \textit{ Type II error}.
\end{itemize}

These four outcomes form the basis for evaluation.

In the multinomial case, \emph{one-vs-rest approach} is commonly used. Each class, observations belonging to one particular class are treated as positive, while all remaining observations are treated as negative. This reduces the problem to a series of binary classifications, allowing the same evaluation criteria to be used.

The results are typicaly summarized using a \textit{confusion matrix}, as shown in \ref{tab:confusion_matrix}:


\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & \text{Predicted Positive} & \text{Predicted Negative} \\
\hline
\text{Actual Positive} 
    & \text{True Positive} 
    & \text{False Negative (Type II Error)} \\
\text{Actual Negative} 
    & \text{False Positive (Type I Error)} 
    & \text{True Negative}
\end{array}
\]
\caption{Confusion matrix.}
\label{tab:confusion_matrix}
\end{table}

From the confusion matrix, several performance measures can be derived: \\

\textbf{Accuracy}, sometimes reffered to as \emph{rand index}, or \emph{rand accuracy} is defined as the proportion of correctly classified observations among all observations, as shown in Equation~\ref{eq:accuracy}. 

\begin{equation}
    \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
\end{equation}

Although intuitive, accuracy can be misleading in the presence of imbalanced class distributions. For example, consider a disease with a low prevalence of $0.5\%$. A naive model that classifies every observation as negative achieves an accuracy of 99.5\%. Despite this high value, the model fails to identify any positive cases. This shows that accuracy alone might not not be sufficient for evaluation of the model. 

\textbf{Precision} measures the proportion of correctly predicted positive samples among all predicted positive samples:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Recall (Sensitivity)} is proportion of correctly identified positive samples out of all actual positives: 
\begin{equation}
    \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Specificity} measures the proportion of correctly identified negative samples out of all actual negatives:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}
\end{equation}

\textbf{F1 score} is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:
\begin{equation}
    F1 = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
\end{equation}


\textit{The true positive rate (TPR)} corresponds to recall. \textit{The false positive rate (FPR)} is defined as $1 - \textit{specificity}$ and quantifies the proportion of negative samples incorrectly classified as positive.

\paragraph{Curve-based Metrics \\}
The metrics above depend on a fixed \emph{classification threshold}, and they do not capture how the model behaves across all possible thresholds. To address this problem, so called \emph{curve-based methods} are used.

\textit{The Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC curve)} plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate for different threshold values. It describes the trade-off between correctly identifying positive samples and incorrectly classifying negative samples. An example of an ROC curve can be seen in \textcolor{green}{Figure~X.X}. Since the ROC curve reflects both TPR and FPR across all possible choices of the cut-off point \(p\), it represents a robust measure of the quality of binary classifiers. The most widely used scalar summary of the ROC curve is the \textit{Area Under the Curve (AUC)}, which corresponds to the total area lying below it. Clearly, \( \text{AUC} \in [0,1] \), and higher values indicate a better classifier.

In some applications, only a specific part of the \emph{ROC curve} is relevant. \emph{The AUC (pAUC)} measures the area under the curve over a restricted range of FPR values, typically chosen to reflect the region where the classifier is expected to operate in practice. For example, in applications where a very low false positive rate is required, one may compute the pAUC over the interval $\mathrm{FPR} \in [0, \alpha]$ for a small $\alpha$.

\emph{The Youden index} is another useful measure for threshold selection. It is defined as the sum of sensitivity and specificity minus one, and is often used to identify an optimal operating point.

\emph{The precision–recall (PR) curve} plots precision against recall across thresholds. It is particularly useful for imbalanced datasets, where ROC curves may present an overly optimistic view of performance.

\emph{The area under the PR curve (AUPR)} summarizes the PR curve into a single value and reflects the model’s ability to identify positive samples when they are rare.

\subsection{Criteria for Regression Tasks}
\label{sec:criteria-regression-tasks}
In regression tasks, we are supposed to predict the target variable that takes continous values. To assess prediction performance, several commonly used metrics can be used.
Let $y_i$ denote the observed value of the dependent variable, $\hat{y}_i$ the predicted value, and $n$ the number of observations.

\textbf{Mean Squared Error (MSE)} measures the average squared difference between observed and predicted values. Because the errors are squared, it penalizes larger errors more strongly than the smaller ones. For this reason, MSE is widely used as a training loss function.

\begin{equation}
    \text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
\end{equation}

\textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)} is the square root of MSE. It is expressed in the same units as predicted variable, which makes interpretation more intuitive. It, however, remains sensitive to outliers.

\begin{equation}
    \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 }
\end{equation}

\textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE)} calculates the average absolute difference between observed and predicted values. Unlike MSE, it is less affected by extreme observations and provides a more robust measure of typical error size.

\begin{equation}
    \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
\end{equation}

\textbf{$\mathbf{R}^\mathbf{2}$} represents the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the model. Typically, $R^2 \in [0,1]$, with higher values indicating a better fit.
 Although commonly used, it tends to increase as more predictors are added, even if they do not contribute meaningful information; however, adjusted versions of the metric can be used to account for model complexity. $\mathrm{R}^2$ is common in linear models but not in neural networks, where MSE or RMSE better evaluate prediction performance and are suitable as a loss function.

\begin{equation}
    R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}
\end{equation}

In the context of neural networks, metrics such as MSE and RMSE are not only useful for evaluating overall prediction performance, but also guiding the training process. They are often used as a \emph{loss functions} during network optimization, helping adjust weights and biases to minimize prediction error.

\section{Information Theory}
\emph{Information theory} is a branch of applied mathematics focused on quantifying the amount of information contained in a signal or random variable. The main idea is that observing unlikely events provides more information than observing likely ones. This framework is useful for feature selection, since it allows measuring how informative a feature is with respect to a target variable.

\textbf{\\Self-information\\}
Let $X$ be a discrete random variable with possible values $x \in \mathcal{X}$, and let $P(x)$ denote the probability mass function of $X$. The information content of a single outcome $x$ is measured by \emph{self-information}:
\begin{equation}
    I(x) = -\log P(x).
\end{equation}

Here, $P(x)$ is the probability of observing the event $x$, and the logarithm is typically taken in base $2$ or the natural logarithm, depending on the context. 

This definition satisfies three intuitive properties \cite{goodfellow2016}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Events with high probability have low information content.
    \item Events with probability equal to 1 carry zero information.
    \item Independent events have additive information.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{\\Entropy\\}
\emph{Self-information} deals only with a single outcome. To measure uncertainty over an entire probability distribution of random variable $X$, \emph{Shannon entropy} is used \cite{goodfellow2016}.

It is defined as expected value of \emph{self-information}:
\begin{equation}
    H(X) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[-\log P(x)] = - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i)\log P(x_i).
\end{equation}
Here, $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\} \subset \mathcal{X}$ are the possible outcomes of $X$, and $P(x_i)$ is the probability of outcome $x_i$. The entropy $H(X)$ can be interpreted as the uncertainty associated with $X$. For continuous random variables, the analogous concept is called \emph{differential entropy}, where the summation is replaced by an integral over the probability density function.

Note: In practice, expressions of the form $0 \log 0$ may appear in the computation of entropy and related quantities. By convention in information theory, these terms are defined using the limit
\[
\lim_{x \to 0} x \log x = 0,
\]
and are therefore taken to be equal to zero. This ensures that entropy remains well-defined even when some events have zero probability.

\textbf{\\Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence\\}
To compare two probability distributions $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ defined over the same probability space $\mathcal{X}$, the \emph{Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence} is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} \left[ \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \right]
    = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[\log P(x) - \log Q(x)].
\end{equation}

Here, $P(x)$ is the true distribution and $Q(x)$ is an approximation. The expectation is taken with respect to $P(x)$. The KL divergence measures how different $Q$ is from $P$. KL divergence is always non-negative and equals zero only when the two distributions are identical. It is not symmetric, so it is not a true distance metric.

\textbf{\\Cross-entropy\\}
Closely related to KL divergence is the \emph{cross-entropy}, which is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    H(P, Q) = - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[\log Q(x)],
\end{equation}
where $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are defined as above. Cross-entropy can be decomposed as:
\begin{equation}
    H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_{KL}(P \parallel Q),
\end{equation}
where $H(P)$ is the entropy of the true distribution $P$. Since $H(P)$ does not depend on $Q$, minimizing cross-entropy with respect to $Q$ is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence.

\textbf{\\Mutual Information\\}
\emph{Mutual information} quantifies the amount of information shared between two random variables $X$ and $Y$. It measures how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about the other. It is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    I(X;Y) = \mathbb{E}_{x,y}\left[\log \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)}\right],
\end{equation}
where $X$ and $Y$ are random variables with joint probability distribution $P(x,y)$, and marginal distributions $P(x)$ and $P(y)$. The expectation is taken with respect to the joint distribution of $X$ and $Y$.

Mutual information is symmetric and non-negative. It equals zero if and only if the variables are independent. In feature selection, it is commonly used to measure the relevance of a feature with respect to a target variable.

\textbf{\\Redundancy\\}
\emph{Redundancy} measures the overlap of information between a feature and a set of other features. Given a feature $f$ and a set of variables $S$, redundancy can be defined as the average mutual information between $f$ and each variable in $S$:
\begin{equation}
    R(f,S) = \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{x_i \in S} I(f,x_i),
\end{equation}
where $f$ is a feature represented as a random variable, $S = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{|S|}\}$ is a set of random variables (features), and $I(f,x_i)$ denotes the mutual information between $f$ and $x_i$.

A high redundancy value indicates that the feature $f$ shares similar information with other features in the set, which may reduce its usefulness in a feature selection process.



\section{Statistical Learning Theory for ANNs}
\label{sec:generalization-neural-networks}

\subsection{The No Free Lunch Theorem}
The \emph{No Free Lunch theorem} states that, averaged over all possible data-generating distributions, all learning algorithms perform equally well. In other words, no algorithm is universally superior when considering all possible problems.

For ANNs this means, that no architecture or training method is universally optimal. Learning depends on inductive bias (e.g., architectural choices, regularization techniques), within which chosen model implicitly encodes assumptions about the underlying data.

\subsection{Hypothesis space and VC Dimension}
In statistical learning theory, the \emph{hypothesis space} is the set of all functions that a learning algorithm can select from when fitting a model to data. Formally, given an input space $\mathcal{X}$ and an output space $\mathcal{Y}$, a hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$ is a collection of functions $h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$.

In the context of artificial neural networks (ANNs), the hypothesis space is determined by the architecture of the network, including the number of layers, the number of neurons per layer, the type of activation functions, and the connectivity pattern. For a fixed architecture, the hypothesis space consists of all functions that can be represented by varying the network parameters (weights and biases). Thus, training an ANN can be interpreted as searching within a high-dimensional hypothesis space to find a function that minimizes a given loss function.

For deep neural networks, the hypothesis space is typically extremely large due to to the multiple layers and non-linear activation functions \cite{goodfellow2016}.  


As an illustrative example, consider the task of recognizing handwritten digits from the MNIST dataset, which contains $70,000$ grayscale images of digits $0$--$9$, and with image dimension of $28 \times 28$ \cite{lecun1998, mnistdataset}.  

A fully connected neural network with one hidden layer of 128 neurons can be used for this task. With 784 input neurons, 128 hidden neurons, and 10 output neurons, the total number of parameters is given by 

\[
\underbrace{784 \times 128}_{\text{input-to-hidden weights}} + 
\underbrace{128}_{\text{hidden biases}} + 
\underbrace{128 \times 10}_{\text{hidden-to-output weights}} + 
\underbrace{10}_{\text{output biases}} \approx 100{,}000 \text{.}
\]

Each combination of these values defines a distinct hypothesis, illustrating that even a relatively simple architecture can have a large \textit{(expressive) capacity}, which is its ability to represent a wide range of functions or patterns in the data.

To formalize the notion of model capacity, one commonly used concept is the \emph{Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension}. The VC dimension of a hypothesis space \( \mathcal{H} \) is defined as the largest number of points that can be \emph{shattered} by \( \mathcal{H} \). A set of points is said to be \emph{shattered} if, for every possible labeling of these points, there exists a hypothesis in \( \mathcal{H} \) that correctly classifies them.

\subsection{Generalization in ANNs}

However, such a large hypothesis space can lead to poor generalization. \textit{Generalization} refers to the ability of a learning algorithm to perform well on previously unseen data drawn from the same underlying distribution as the training data. While a model is optimized using a finite dataset, the ultimate goal is to learn a function that approximates the true data-generating process rather than fitting the observed samples.

The \textit{generalisation error} is the expected loss of a model over the true data distribution. It can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\left[ \mathcal{L}\big(\hat{f}_{\mathbf{\theta}}(x), y\big) \right],
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the data-generating distribution, $L(\cdot)$ is the loss function (defined previously), and $\hat{f}_{\mathbf{\theta}}(x)$ is the model prediction. In practice, since the true distribution is unknown, the generalisation error is approximated using a finite dataset.

\textbf{\\Training Error and Test Error\\}
The \emph{training error} is the average loss computed on the training dataset. It reflects how well the model fits the data it was trained on.

The \emph{test error} is the average loss computed on a separate dataset not used during training. This dataset is assumed to be representative of the same underlying distribution but is not used in parameter estimation.

While the training error is directly optimised during learning, the test error serves as an estimator of the generalisation error. A small training error does not necessarily imply good generalisation, as the model may have memorised the training data rather than learned the underlying patterns.

\textbf{\\Noise\\}
\emph{Noise} refers to the random variability or errors present in the data that cannot be explained by the underlying functional relationship between inputs and outputs. Noise can arise from measurement errors, incomplete information, or inherent randomness in the data-generating process.

Noise limits the achievable performance of any model. Even an optimal model cannot perfectly predict outputs if the data contains irreducible noise. In practice, excessive fitting of noise leads to poor generalisation.

\textbf{\\Overfitting and underfitting\\}
When the model fits the training data too poorly it said to underfit. \textit{Underfitting} occurs when the chosen hypothesis is too simple relative to the data distribution; this prevents the model from capturing essential patterns and leads to high training error.

Conversely, when the model captures not only essential patterns, but also noise, it is said to overfit. \textit{Overfitting} arises when the hypothesis is more complex than the data on which the model was trained and can lead to high \textit{generalization error}, or also called \textit{test error}.

The \textit{generalisation gap} is defined as the difference between the training error and the test (generalization) error. It measures how well the model transfers learned patterns to unseen data.

A large generalization gap indicates overfitting, while a small gap suggests that the model generalizes well. In deep learning, monitoring the generalization gap is a common diagnostic tool.

\textbf{\\Bias-variance trade-off\\}
Important concept tightly linked to overfitting and underfitting is the \emph{bias-variance trade-off}. Let 
\(\hat f(x)\) denote the estimator learned from data, and let \(f(x)\) be the true underlying function we are estimating. The \emph{expected prediction error} at a point \(x\) can be decomposed as
\[
\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x) - f(x))^2\big]
=
\underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}
+
\sigma^2,
\]
where \(\sigma^2\) is the irreducible noise in the data.

The bias term quantifies the error due to restrictive assumptions in the model. A model with high bias fails to approximate complex patterns and tends to underfit.

The variance, which underlies the problem of overfitting, increases with model complexity. As complexity grows, bias decreases while variance rises. A larger number of parameters allows the model to capture more complex relationships between predictors and the response.

Neural networks, because of their high capacity and extremely large hypothesis space, can achieve low bias but often exhibit large variance, especially when the number of parameters is high relative to the sample size. 

\textbf{\\Validation\\}
To estimate generalization performance and tune model hyperparameters, the dataset is typically partitioned into three subsets:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Training set}: used to fit the model parameters,
    \item \emph{Validation set}: used to select models and tune hyperparameters,
    \item \emph{Test set}: used for final evaluation of generalization performance.
\end{itemize}

This separation is essential to obtain an unbiased estimate of the test error and to avoid information leakage from the evaluation data into the training process.

A typical workflow proceeds as follows. First, the dataset is split into training, validation, and test subsets. The model is then trained on the training set for a given choice of hyperparameters. Its performance is evaluated on the validation set, which is used to guide hyperparameter tuning and model selection. This process may be repeated multiple times for different configurations. After selecting the final model, its generalization performance is assessed once on the test set.

However, when the available dataset is limited, a simple split may lead to high variance in performance estimates. In such cases, cross-validation is commonly used. The dataset is divided into \( K \) disjoint subsets (folds), and the model is trained \( K \) times. In each iteration, one fold is used as a validation set, while the remaining \( K - 1 \) folds are used for training.

The cross-validation risk is defined as
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{CV}} =
    \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{\lvert D_k \rvert}
    \sum_{(x_i, y_i) \in D_k}
    L\big(\hat{f}^{(-k)}(x_i), y_i\big),
\end{equation}
where \( D_k \) denotes the \( k \)-th fold, \( \lvert D_k \rvert \) its size, \( L \) the loss function, and \( \hat{f}^{(-k)} \) the model trained without using the \( k \)-th fold.

Cross-validation provides a more robust estimate of the generalization error compared to a single train-test split, as it reduces the dependence on a particular partition of the data. 

\subsection{Regularisation Techniques}
Deep neural networks are highly expressive models with large hypothesis spaces, which makes them prone to overfitting. To improve generalisation performance, it is necessary to control model complexity and prevent the model from fitting noise in the training data. This is achieved through \emph{regularisation techniques}, which impose constraints on the learning process.

\textbf{\\Parameter norm penalties\\}
One common approach to address problem of overfitting is by adding a penalty term to a loss function, which effectively controls model complexity.

The general form of a regularized cost functions is:

\begin{equation}
    L(h) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(h(x_i), y_i\big) + \lambda \Omega(h),
\end{equation}

where $L(h)$ is the objective function to be minimized, $\ell(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the sample loss measuring the discrepancy between predictions $h(x_i)$ and true outputs $y_i$, $\Omega(h)$ is a regularization term controlling model complexity, $\lambda \ge 0$ is a regularization parameter balancing fit and complexity, and $n$ is the number of training samples. The regularization term is typically defined in terms of the model parameters by writing $h \equiv h_{\theta}$ with $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$.

The regularization term $\Omega(h)$ is typically defined in terms of the model parameters. Let $h \equiv h_{\theta}$, where $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ denotes the parameter vector of the model.

\textbf{\\L1 Regularization (Lasso)\\}

Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) uses an L1 penalty::
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(\theta) = \|\theta\|_1 = \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\theta_j|.
\end{equation}

L1 regularization promotes sparsity in the parameter vector, meaning that some coefficients are driven exactly to zero, which can also be interpreted as an implicit form of feature selection.

\textbf{\\L2 Regularization (Ridge)\\}
In L2 regularization (also known as Ridge or Weight decay), the penalty is given by the squared Euclidean norm of the parameter vector:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(\theta) = \|\theta\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{p} \theta_j^2.
\end{equation}

L2 regularization encourages small parameter values and tends to distribute weights more evenly across features.

\textbf{\\Elastic Net regularization\\}
Elastic Net combines both L1 and L2 penalties. The regularization term is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(\theta) = \alpha \|\theta\|_1 + (1 - \alpha)\|\theta\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controls the trade-off between the L1 and L2 components - it promotes sparsity and feature selection through the L1 component, while the L2 component improves stability and encourages grouping of correlated features.

\textbf{\\Early stoping\\}
Early stopping is a regularisation technique based on monitoring model performance on a validation set during training. Instead of training the model until convergence, the process is stopped when the validation error starts to increase, indicating the onset of overfitting.

In practice, the data are split into training and validation sets, the model is trained iteratively (e.g., via gradient descent), and its performance on the validation set is evaluated after each epoch; training is terminated once the validation error does not decrease for a predefined number of iterations, to which we refer as patience parameter.

Early stopping can be interpreted as implicit regularisation, since iterative optimisation tends to increase model complexity over time, and stopping early prevents the model from reaching overly complex solutions.

\textbf{\\Dropout\\}
Dropout is a stochastic regularisation technique in which neurons are randomly removed during training: each neuron is retained with probability $p$ and set to zero otherwise. This reduces encourages more robust representations. At test time, all neurons are used with appropriately scaled outputs. Dropout can be viewed as an approximation to averaging predictions over many network architectures.

\textbf{\\Data Augmentation\\}
Data augmentation increases the effective size of the training dataset by applying transformations to existing data points. In image tasks, this may include rotations, translations, or scaling; in other domains, appropriate transformations depend on the structure of the data.

By exposing the model to a wider variety of inputs, data augmentation reduces overfitting and improves generalisation without explicitly modifying the model or loss function.
